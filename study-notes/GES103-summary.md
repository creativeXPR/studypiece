## INTRODUCTION — The Object and Nature of the Social Sciences (A.S. Jegede)

### Definitions of Social Science
- **Social science** = the study of human relationships within the socio-cultural environment. 🔑
- **Charles Beard:** body of knowledge and thought pertaining to **human affairs** as distinguished from **sticks, stones, stars and physical objects** (Kochhar 1984). 🔑
- **James High:** bodies of learning that recognise the **simultaneous and mutual action of physical and non-physical stimuli** which produce social reaction (Singh 2008).
- **Hunt and Colander (2008):** simply "the **study of human relations**".
- Components of social behaviour studied: economic, political, cultural behaviour, traditions, customs, social institutions, religious faiths, ethical/value patterns.

### Evolution
- Social science is a **product of the 18th century** and became a field of study in higher institutions. 🔑
- A **by-product of modernisation, industrialisation, renaissance, urbanisation, growth of science** (Barker 1967; Ross 1991).
- Historical triggers: Renaissance (Italy & Europe), **American War of Independence 1776**, **French Revolution 1789**, **Industrial Revolution (from 1767)**, new forms of capitalism.
- Developed as a response to the need to **understand modernity** (Ross 1991).
- Exemplary founding texts 🔑:
  - **Montesquieu** – *Spirit of the Laws* (**1748**)
  - **Adam Smith** – *Wealth of Nations* (**1776**)
  - **Condorcet** – *Outline of an Historical View of the Progress of the Human Mind* (**1795**)
  - **J.G. Herder** – *Ideas towards a Philosophy of History* (**1784–91**)
- Industrialisation brought problems (disease, alienation, worker exploitation); science brought germ warfare, atomic weapons, hazardous chemicals. Today: **climate change → desertification in northern Nigeria → communal conflicts** over shrinking land.

### What is "scientific" about Social Science?
- Social science = **disciplined and systematic study** of society, its institutions, and how/why people behave as individuals and groups (Hargittai 2009).
- To be scientific: **systematic, stepwise** knowledge acquisition; knowledge must be **verifiable** and open to verification at all times, meeting standards of the scientific community.
- Difference from hard sciences: variables in hard sciences can be held constant, but humans change → hence the use of **"ceteris paribus"** = "**all things being equal**" (Galavotti 2003). 🔑
- Critics (Gareau 1987): society is not susceptible to scientific study.
- 🔑 Example: In the early **1980s the British Government renamed the Social Science Research Council** to the **Economic and Social Research Council** → "if it's social it can't be scientific!" (**Halloran 1998**).

### Scope of Social Science
- As wide as the discipline; concerned with human relations; a product of history and development.
- Uses knowledge of the past to build **development models** for human welfare.
- Studies **man-made institutions** (family, marriage, politics, economy) which regulate behaviour — "the freedom of an individual ends where another's begins".
- Transmits **societal values** across generations; enables prediction of behaviour related to new technologies (e.g. **cybercrime**); studies **internationalism/globalisation**.

### Why Study Social Science?
- Solves practical problems — social, economic, political, health.
- Most health problems are products of human behaviour (e.g. **HIV** in Africa/Asia, late 1990s–2000s) → reason **UI introduced GES 107** (HIV causes, prevention, management). 🔑
- Develops efficient **citizens of democracy**, forms social character, aids moral progress, institution/community development, **international viewpoint**, decision making.

### Objectives of Social Science
- Provides knowledge of natural and social environment; develops human qualities via education, learning and socialisation; makes learners know their duties to society.
- Develops **imagination, critical thinking, reasoning**; desirable attitudes; ability to **compare, analyse, summarise, evaluate**.
- Develops personality, **cooperation, morality, universal brotherhood** — "the hallmark of the Ibadan School of Social Sciences".

### The Ibadan School of Social Sciences (IBSSS) 🔑
- Began with the **Department of Economics in 1958**.
- **Geography** started earlier (**1948**) but sat in Arts and Science until it joined Social Sciences in **1967**.
- Genealogy: **Political Science grew out of Economics → Sociology out of Political Science → Law and Psychology out of Sociology.**
- The Faculty has **six departments**. Two faculties grew out of it: **Faculty of Economics** and **Faculty of Environmental Design and Management**.
- IBSSS has **"decolonised"** social theory — universal methods but **culturally relevant** curriculum.

### Conclusion
- Social scientists must detach from **common sense** ("person on the street") but not so detached that they impose the **expert's perspective** → must be **objective**.

**Likely questions:** Define social science (any 2 scholars); events that gave rise to social science; meaning of *ceteris paribus*; why social science is "scientific"; history of IBSSS; objectives of social science.

## CHAPTER 3 — Political Socialisation and Political Participation (I. Johnson & A.L. Alabi)

### Key Definitions
- **Socialisation:** process whereby an individual learns to adjust to a group/society and behave in a manner approved by it; a lifelong process (Encyclopaedia Britannica 2010).
- **Political socialisation:** the **deliberate inculcation of political information, values and practices** by instructional agents formally and informally saddled with that responsibility. 🔑
- **Almond & Powell (1992):** "all political learning, formal and informal, deliberate and unplanned, at every stage of the developmental cycle…" including non-political learning that affects political behaviour.
- **Basic hypothesis:** no political system can attain/remain integrated unless members share **political knowledge, values and attitudes** → political socialisation is a means of building **political cohesion**. 🔑

### Content of Political Socialisation
- Its intent/content is the **political culture**. 🔑
- **Gabriel Almond (Almond & Coleman 1960:27):** "Political socialisation is the process of **induction into the political culture**. Its end product is a set of **attitudes, cognitions, value standards and feelings** towards the political system, its roles and role incumbents."
- Political action is determined by **objective situations** and **predispositions** (goals, rules of the game, legitimacy, group loyalties, views on human nature, orientation to authority).
- The whole personality cycle (childhood → adulthood) must be covered: child's image of government, regime norms, nationality, democratic orientations, political vocabulary and ideology, **elite socialisation/re-socialisation**.

### Five Utilities (Functions) of Political Socialisation (Dennis 1973) 🔑
1. **Stress-absorber** — avoids/reduces stress on essential variables so the system continues.
2. **Output side** — ensures government decisions are **acceptable and binding**.
3. **Input side** — streamlines/controls the **volume and variety of demands** so the system isn't overburdened.
4. Prepares members for roles that **convert inputs into binding outputs**.
5. Generates at least a **minimal level of positive support** for basic political objects.
- It is a tool for **national acculturation**.
- Distinguish: **politically relevant personality development** vs **specifically political learning**, which includes learning connected with (i) the **citizen role** (partisanship, ideology, participation), (ii) the **subject role** (loyalty, authority, legitimacy), (iii) **recruitment to specialised roles** (bureaucrat, party functionary, legislator).
- **Civic training** (subset of acculturation) becomes deliberate policy when instability is perceived — e.g. **USA** (doctored civic training in schools) and **Russia** (state takes responsibility for character training).

### Agencies/Agents of Political Socialisation 🔑
1. **Parents/Family** — the **primary** agency; child's **first cognitive map** of the world.
2. **School system** — formal learning (Government, Civics, debates, class leadership, unionism).
3. **Peer group** — similar age/income/status/interests; friends, colleagues, clubs.
4. **Mass media and social media** — newspapers, magazines, TV, radio, internet; government often interferes in what they disseminate.
5. **Voluntary associations and interest groups** — Boys' Brigade, Girls' Guide, Boy Scouts, Red Cross; professional associations, political parties.
(Also: political parties, religious organisations, government institutions.)

### Political Participation
- People participate via **organised political activities** through structures like parties, elections, legislatures, executives and bureaucracies.
- Describes the **relationship between state and society**.

### Six Avenues/Channels of Political Participation 🔑
1. **Elections** — the **foremost** avenue; formal decision-making by which citizens choose public office holders. Restrictions: minimum age, foreigners, mentally incompetent (Kesselman et al. 2007) despite **universal adult suffrage**.
2. **Political parties** — organisations that seek to influence policy by nominating candidates. Activities: campaigns, outreach, protest, ideology, coalitions. Electoral system (e.g. **simple plurality** vs **proportional representation**) shapes the party system. Types: **non-partisan, single-party, multiparty** (non-partisan are very few).
3. **Legislative representation** — "citizens' direct regular voice". Legislature passes laws, approves budgets, scrutinises administration.
   - **Bicameral:** House of Reps (by **population**) and Senate (**equal representation of states**) — Nigeria & USA. A rep serves ~**500,000–700,000** constituents.
   - **Britain:** House of **Commons** (elected) and House of **Lords** (unelected: hereditary peers — duke, marquis, earl, viscount, baron; Archbishops of **Canterbury and York** + **24 senior bishops**; life peers appointed by the Crown on PM's advice; Law Lords).
4. **Interest and pressure groups** — organised associations that lobby to influence policy. **Five categories** 🔑:
   - **Economic interests** (manufacturers, labour congress, trade unions, farmers, professionals)
   - **Cause groups** (religious, veterans, disabled, anti-HIV, anti-abortion)
   - **Public interests** (human rights, consumer protection, environment, Red Cross, Amnesty International)
   - **Private and public institutional interests** (universities, student unions, press, AU, EU, UN)
   - **Non-associational groups** (government departments, agencies, the military)
5. **Public opinion and public protests** — the collection of attitudes/beliefs held by adults on public issues; opinion expressed via mass media, protests organised by pressure groups. They're **extensions of pressure-group activity**.
6. **Public bureaucracy** — civil servants, especially **top civil servants** (an "elite corps" that watches governments come and go). Supposed to be neutral but have **conservative propensities**.
   - **Theory of Representative Bureaucracy:** a workforce that reflects the population (indigeneity, ethnicity, race, sex) ensures all groups' interests are considered (**Bradbury & Kellough 2014**; **Krislov 2012**).

### Voting as Indicator of Political Socialisation and Participation
- Voting is the **most visible indicator** of political socialisation and participation. 🔑
- **Merkl (1988):** democracy has a **natural correction process** — dissatisfied majorities can replace rulers.
- **Voting age population** = adult group from census figures. **Eligible/registered voters** = those of voting age who registered.
- **Voter turnout** = fundamental quality of fair elections; low turnout = disengagement.
- Two key measures: % of actual voters to **registered voters**, and % to **voting age population**.

**Nigerian turnout figures** 🔑

| Election | % of registered voters | % of voting-age population |
|---|---|---|
| 1979 (military→civilian) | 35.25% | 44.83% |
| 1983 (civilian→civilian) | 38.94% | 58.23% |
| 1999 (military→civilian) | 52.26% | 57.36% |
| 2003 | **69.08%** (highest) | 65.33% |

- Dec 1983 coup → military rule **1984–1999** (16 years). **1993** presidential election (presumed won by **Chief M.K.O. Abiola**) annulled by **Gen. Ibrahim Babangida**.
- Average turnout, last 5 elections up to 2016 (International IDEA 2017): **Nigeria 58.13%**, Ghana 74.98%, **South Africa 82.54% (highest)**, France 80.93%, USA 80.04% → Nigeria's is relatively **low**.

### Causes of Voter/Political Apathy in Nigeria
- **Ejue & Ekanem (2011)** — three issues 🔑:
  1. Importance of elections not understood/appreciated.
  2. Voting-age population don't know their **rights as source of power**.
  3. Voters' rights **not protected** by government.
- Underlying: flaws in political socialisation, **mass illiteracy**, **bad governance**.
- **Illiteracy:** population ~200 million, ~35% illiterate (60–100 million). Nigeria belongs to the **E-9** (Bangladesh, Brazil, China, Egypt, India, Indonesia, Mexico, Nigeria, Pakistan) — together >50% of world's illiterates (**Naboli 2013**).
- **Bad governance:** failure to convert oil wealth into development → image of **political fraud**.

### Corrective Modelling — Three Perspectives 🔑
1. **Good governance perspective** — people identify with the system only when it functions in a gratifying way; elites must show competence, sincerity, transparency, accountability.
2. **Agency modelling** — deliberately structure and monitor all agents of socialisation.
3. **Content adjustment** — three areas: **(a) citizenship education, (b) value re-orientation, (c) social mobilisation.** 🔑

#### (a) Citizenship Education
- **Roberts (2006):** democracy can produce bad leaders where voters are illiterate or coerced by **godfathers**.
- Problems: voting by party/ethnicity, underage voting, impersonation, **vote-selling**.
- **Klusmeyer (1996):** citizenship is the **source of all governmental authority**; the social and legal link between individuals and the democratic community.
- Goal: make Nigerians **"responsible citizens"** (being a citizen ≠ being a responsible citizen).
- **Three participatory skills** 🔑: **Interacting** (communication/cooperation), **Monitoring** (tracking leaders), **Influencing** (affecting outcomes).
- **Civic dispositions:** civility, honesty, tolerance, self-restraint, compassion, trust, duty, loyalty, courage, political efficacy, concern for the common good.
- Nigerian civics curricula lack **"practicals"**.

#### (b) Value Re-orientation
- Values = goals people work for; determine where energy goes (**Ugwuegbu 2004**). Values are learned, become internalised, and are **dynamic** (can change). A **value system** = hierarchical rank-ordering of values.
- National value re-orientation = social movement to direct cognitive and behavioural dispositions along chosen lines.
- **History of Nigerian value re-orientation programmes** 🔑:

| Year | Leader | Programme |
|---|---|---|
| 1977 | Gen. Olusegun Obasanjo | **Jaji Declaration** (first public acknowledgement; mere rhetoric) |
| 1982 | Alhaji Shehu Shagari | **National Ethical Revolution**; Ministry of National Guidance |
| 1984 | Gen. Muhammadu Buhari | **War Against Indiscipline (WAI)** — 5 phases: queue culture, work ethics, nationalism/patriotism, corruption/economic sabotage, environmental sanitation |
| 1985 | Gen. Ibrahim Babangida | **National Orientation Movement (NOM)** |
| 1987 | Babangida | **MAMSER** — Directorate for Social Mobilisation, Self-Reliance, Economic Recovery and Social Justice |
| 1993 | (Abacha era) | **National Orientation Agency (NOA)** |
| 2004 | Obasanjo (civilian) | **NEEDS** — National Economic Empowerment and Development Strategy |
| — | Goodluck Jonathan | No distinct programme |

- **Five errors** in government handling 🔑:
  1. **Inconsistent assumptions** (WAI: discipline; NOM: conditioned orientation; NOA: weakened values; NEEDS: crisis/leadership).
  2. **Lack of continuity** — each regime brings its own.
  3. **No empirical database** of Nigerians' values.
  4. **No clear-cut ideology**.
  5. **No value re-orientation for the state** and its institutions (no corporate vision/mission/values).

#### (c) Social Mobilisation
- Erodes old commitments so people become available for new patterns of behaviour.
- **Okafor (2003):** achieving a goal through properly articulated **group action**.
- **Akeredolu-Ale (1989):** to mobilise = commit to productive use for maximum contribution to collective goals.
- **Edward Shils (1968) — two stages** 🔑: (1) **withdrawal/disengagement** from old negative habits; (2) **induction** into new stable patterns of membership and commitment.
- MAMSER came from the **Political Bureau** recommendation; slogan: "mass mobilisation for social justice, self-reliance and economic recovery".
- **MAMSER's faulty assumptions:** that there already existed (1) a **national society**, (2) a **national elite**, (3) **national masses** with common orientation. It underestimated ethnic, religious and competitive cleavages (**Alabi 1990**).
- **Proposal:** a **direct marketing/direct advocacy** strategy (like NGOs) — segment Nigeria into problem groups and deliver tailored solutions (e.g. the HIV patient example).

**Likely questions:** Define political socialisation; list agents; five utilities; avenues of participation; categories of pressure groups; causes of voter apathy; turnout statistics; value re-orientation programmes and their failures; Shils's two stages; three participatory skills.

## CHAPTER 4 — Law, Democracy and Good Governance in Nigeria (O.I. Adejumo)

### Introduction
- **Law (Ugiade 2004):** a body of official rules and regulations (in constitutions, legislation, judicial opinions) used to govern society and control members' behaviour.
- The three concepts are interrelated; the source is the **constitution**.
- Questions raised: the **1999 Constitution** was enacted by the military via a hurriedly assembled **Constitution Debate Co-ordinating Committee** — can it truly claim "**We the People of Nigeria**"? Nigeria's **legitimacy crisis** comes from the state defaulting on its **social contract**.

### What is Law?
- **Black's Law Dictionary (2004):** regime that orders human activities through systematic application of the force of politically organised society.
- **John Austin (1832)** 🔑: "a rule laid down for the guidance of an intelligent being by an intelligent being having power over him" → law is the **command of the sovereign** to the commanded.
- Law is made by authorised bodies and backed by the **coercive power of the state**.
- **Philip Cook (1986):** without law enforcement and sanctions → "a crime wave of unprecedented proportions".
- Constitution: **sovereignty belongs to the people**; **security and welfare** of the people is the **primary responsibility** of government; participation shall be ensured.
- **Duties of citizens** (Constitution): abide by the Constitution and respect the flag, anthem, pledge; enhance Nigeria's name and defend it; respect others' dignity; contribute to the community; assist in maintaining law and order; **declare income honestly and pay tax promptly**.

### Functions of Law 🔑
1. **Maintenance of public order** (Lord Donalds — without law we'd bully one another).
2. **Facilitates co-operative actions** (contracts, unions, intergovernmental cooperation in a federation).
3. **Confers legitimacy** — **Malcolm Shaw (2010)**: legitimacy is a property of a rule that exerts a pull towards compliance; **Ugiagbe (2004)**: law moderates the struggle for power by setting criteria for succession.
4. **Upholds rights and duties** — right is **correlative** to duty (no duty, no right).
5. **Communicates standards** (e.g. Section 36, fair hearing).
- Sources: statutes, decrees, edicts, constitutional enactments as interpreted by courts.

### Law vs Morality
- Violation of law → **sanctions**; violation of morality → **no legal sanction**.
- Example: **adultery/fornication** — morally wrong but not punishable in the **South**; punishable in the **North** under the **Penal Code and Sharia**, as is drinking alcohol. 🔑

### Classification of Law 🔑
- **Substantive law** — defines rights and duties and prohibits wrongs (Criminal and Penal Codes: murder, stealing, etc.).
- **Procedural law** — rules for processing civil and criminal cases (jurisdiction, form of trial, time limits). Protected by **Chapter IV**; **non-compliance can make a trial a nullity**. Found in statutes and **rules of court**.
- **Public law** — relationships within government and between government and individuals (criminal, constitutional, administrative law).
- **Private law** — relationships between persons (contract, family, equity and trusts, torts, labour, land, business law). "Persons" include corporations = **juristic persons** (can sue and be sued).
- **Civil law** — private rights enforced by civil action; originated in **ancient Rome**; no penal sanction (e.g. breach of contract → damages).
- **Criminal law** — defines crimes and punishments. Constitution: no conviction unless the offence is **defined and its penalty prescribed in a written law**. 🔑

### Customary Law 🔑
- **Unwritten** traditional law of a community, recognised as binding; a **source of Nigerian law**; not uniform.
- Yoruba inheritance (intestate): **idi-igi** (distribution through **mothers/wives**) and **ori-ojori** (distribution **per head**). **Benin:** the **eldest son** inherits.
- Characteristics:
  1. Validity comes from **acceptability by the community** — *Eshugbayi Eleko v. Government of Nigeria*: "it is the **assent of the native community** that gives a custom its validity"; *Owonyin v. Omotosho*: "**mirror of accepted usage**".
  2. **Unwritten → flexible** — *Lewis v. Bankole* (**Osborne C.J.**): "one of the most striking features of West African native custom is its **flexibility**".
- Courts enforce it if **not repugnant to natural justice, equity and good conscience** and not incompatible with any law (the **repugnancy test**).

### Constitutional Law
- Part of **public law** (citizen–state relationship).
- **Constitution:** frame/composition of government, its organs, distribution of powers and procedures.
- **Hood Phillips and Jackson** — two senses 🔑: **Abstract** = system of laws, customs and conventions defining organs of state; **Concrete** = the **document** in which the most important laws are ordained.
- A good constitution clearly sets out the structure of the **legislature, executive, judiciary** → **separation of powers**.
- Written constitutions are **entrenched** (amended only by special procedure); the people are the **constituent power**.

### Classification of Constitutions 🔑
1. **Written vs Unwritten**
   - Written: Nigeria, USA, India, South Africa — a deliberate creation; **S.14(2)**: "sovereignty belongs to the people of Nigeria…".
   - Unwritten: **Britain, New Zealand** — an "organic growth"; UK statutes include **Magna Carta 1215, Petition of Rights 1628, Bill of Rights 1688, Act of Settlement 1700**, Parliament Acts **1911 & 1949**, Statute of Westminster 1931, etc.
2. **Rigid vs Flexible**
   - **Rigid:** special amendment procedure — 1999 Constitution & US Constitution. **Section 9(2):** amendment needs **two-thirds of all members of each House** of the National Assembly **and** approval by the Houses of Assembly of **two-thirds of all the States**. (**Section 8** covers creation of states.)
   - **Flexible:** amended by ordinary legislation — **Clifford 1922, Richards 1946, Macpherson 1951** (written but flexible).
3. **Monarchical vs Republican** — monarch vs president as head; little practical significance (ceremonial in **1960** and **1963** constitutions respectively).
4. **Presidential vs Parliamentary**
   - **Presidential:** people elect both executive and legislature; a legislator appointed minister **forfeits** their seat (USA; Nigeria **since 1979**).
   - **Parliamentary:** executive (prime minister) comes from the legislature's majority party; ministers must be/become members of the legislature (UK; Nigeria under **1960 & 1963**).
5. **Federal vs Unitary** — Federal: powers shared among "equals" (USA, Nigeria, India). Unitary: hierarchical; other tiers only administrative (**Britain, The Gambia**).
6. **Confederation** — central government **weaker and subordinate** to the component units (vs federation where the centre is stronger).

### Historical Development of Nigerian Constitutions 🔑
- **1914 Lugard → 1922 Clifford → 1946 Richards → 1951 Macpherson → 1954 Lyttelton** (federal; **3 regions** — North, East, West + federal territory of **Lagos**).
- **1960 Independence Constitution**; **1963 Republican Constitution** (created the **Mid-Western Region** → 4 regions).
- **1966** coup suspended the 1963 Constitution → decrees and edicts.
- **1979 Constitution — five-stage transition:**
  1. **Constitution Drafting Committee (Sept 1975)** — 2 per state + learned experts. Military's commitments: federal system with rule of law and fundamental rights; truly national parties; **executive presidential system**; independent judiciary; corrective institutions (**Corrupt Practices Tribunal, Public Complaints Bureau**); restriction on state creation.
  2. **State creation, April 1976** — **12 → 19 states**; common local government framework.
  3. **Constituent Assembly, Aug 1977** (**Decree No. 50 of 1977**) — elected, nominated and CDC members.
  4–5. **FEDECO**, lifting ban on politics, party/voter registration, elections; **handover on 1 October 1979**.
- **Features of the 1979 Constitution:**
  - Federal system modelled on the **USA**; **three tiers** (federal, state, local).
  - **Exclusive Legislative List** (federal only) and **Concurrent List** (federal + state); **residual matters** go to states (**Akande 1982**).
  - **Chapter II – Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy** (first time) — **six justifications** from the CDC: imposes duties on the state; yardstick to judge governments; defines national goals; reminder of unity; removes them from political controversy; acknowledges values change.
  - Objectives listed: political, economic, social, educational, foreign policy, environmental, culture, mass media obligations, national ethics, citizens' duties.
  - 🔑 Chapter II is **non-justiciable** — **Section 6(6)(c)**: courts cannot enforce it.
  - **S.4** legislative powers (Senate + House of Reps + State Assemblies); **S.5** executive powers (President + Governors); **S.6** judicial powers.
  - **Chapter IV — Fundamental Human Rights.**

### Fundamental Human Rights (Chapter IV, 1979 & 1999)
- **Ajomo (2003):** rights human beings enjoy **by virtue of their humanity**.
- US Declaration of Independence: "all men are created equal… **life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness**".
- Founded on **natural law** (a higher, unchangeable law); natural rights are personal and **cannot be waived**; formulation dates to the late 18th-century **American and French revolutions**.

| Right | Section (1999) | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Life | **S.33** | Not absolute — exceptions: court death sentence; reasonable force in defence of person/property, lawful arrest or preventing escape, suppressing riot/insurrection/mutiny |
| Dignity of human person | **S.34** | No torture, slavery or forced labour; exceptions: armed forces, police, **NYSC**, communal duties (e.g. monthly sanitation) |
| Personal liberty | **S.35** | Exceptions: court sentence, failure to obey a court order, reasonable suspicion of crime, minors (education/welfare), infectious disease/unsound mind/addicts/vagrants, immigration/extradition. **Right to remain silent**; told grounds of arrest **in writing within 24 hours** in a language understood; **compensation and public apology** for unlawful detention |
| Fair hearing | **S.36** | Widest right. Maxims 🔑: ***nemo judex in causa sua*** (no one should be a judge in their own cause); ***audi alteram partem*** (hear the other side). Ss.1–3 civil; Ss.4–12 criminal |
| Private & family life | **S.37** | Privacy of homes, correspondence, telephone |
| Thought, conscience, religion | **S.38** | Can change religion; no forced religious instruction; **no right to join a secret society** |
| Expression & press | **S.39** | Cf. **Art. 19 UDHR**; not a licence for false news |
| Peaceful assembly & association | **S.40** | Must be peaceful and for a lawful purpose |
| Movement | **S.41** | Restriction allowed for extradition |
| Freedom from discrimination | **S.42** | Community, ethnicity, origin, sex, religion, politics, **circumstances of birth** |
| Own immovable property | **S.43** | Anywhere in Nigeria |
| Compulsory acquisition | **S.44** | **Prompt compensation**, set by a court/tribunal |

- **Fair hearing requirements (S.36):** public trial; independent and impartial court; **presumption of innocence**; trial within reasonable time; informed promptly of the charge; adequate time to prepare a defence; counsel of one's choice; examine witnesses; **free interpreter**; **no retroactive offence/penalty**; **no double jeopardy**; **not compelled to testify**; offence must be in written law.
- **Limitations (S.45):** rights in **ss.37–41** may be restricted for **defence, public order, public morality, public health** (must be "reasonably justifiable in a democratic society"). Rights in **ss.33 and 35** may be derogated from only in **war or a declared emergency (S.305)**. 🔑

### Constitutional Powers of the People 🔑
- **Vote** in free elections; for the presidential election the **whole federation is one constituency**.
- **Recall** — voters can remove **legislators** before their term ends.
- **Impeachment** — the legislature removes the **President, Vice-President, Governor, Deputy Governor** on behalf of the people (they **cannot be recalled**).
- **Referendum petition** — voters veto unwanted laws (**not yet used in Nigeria**).

### Criminal Law
- Defines crimes and punishments; includes **substantive criminal law** and **criminal procedure**.
- **Crime:** an act or omission rendering a person liable to punishment under the Criminal Code or any law.
- **Two elements** 🔑: ***actus reus*** (guilty act) + ***mens rea*** (guilty mind). Exception: **strict liability** offences (e.g. **drink driving**).
- **Classes of offences:** **Felony** — punishable by death or **3 years+** (murder, treason, armed robbery, terrorism, kidnapping); **Misdemeanour** — **6 months to under 3 years**; **Simple offences** — all others.

### Defences to Crimes 🔑
- **Alibi** (not at the scene); **insanity**.
- **Intoxication** — generally **no defence** under the Criminal Code, except (a) **involuntary** (caused by another's malicious/negligent act), or (b) it caused insanity. Under the Penal Code: only if involuntary.
- **Immature age:** **under 7** = not criminally responsible; **under 12** = not responsible unless capacity to know is proved; **male under 12** presumed incapable of carnal knowledge.
- **Duress**; **mistake of fact** (e.g. taking another's coat thinking it's yours) — but **mistake/ignorance of law is no defence**.
- **Self-defence** — reasonable force, including deadly force against imminent death/serious harm.

### The Police 🔑
- **S.214(1)** 1999 Constitution establishes **one Police Force**. **Police Act CAP 359 (1990), S.4** duties: prevention and detection of crime, apprehension of offenders, preservation of law and order, protection of life and property, enforcement of laws, military duties as required.
- **Arrest** needs **probable cause**; no handcuffs unless violence/escape is feared or ordered by a court.
- **Warrant of arrest:** written authority from a court; complaint **on oath**; dated, signed by a judge/magistrate; can be issued **any day including Sunday/public holidays**; **not executed in a legislative house without the President's permission**; valid until executed or cancelled.
- **Arrest without warrant:** offence in the officer's presence; reasonable suspicion of an **indictable offence**.
- **Private person may arrest** if someone: commits an indictable offence in their view; is reasonably suspected of a **felony**; is suspected of a **misdemeanour by night**. Must hand over to police **without delay**.
- Suspect must be brought to court **within 24 hours**, otherwise granted **bail** (unless a capital/serious offence). **Police bail is free**.
- **Search of a person:** a woman is searched **by a woman officer**.
- **Search of premises:** needs a **search warrant**, except customs officers (**Customs and Excise Management Act, CAP 84, s.147(1)**) and a justice of the peace (**CPC s.85**).

### Democracy
- **Black's Law Dictionary:** government by the people, directly or through elected representatives.
- From Greek (**5th century BC**): ***demos*** = common people, ***kratos*** = power. 🔑
- **Two schools (Janda, Berry & Goldman 1999):** **Procedural** — *how* decisions are made; **Substantive** — *what* government does.
- **Procedural theory, three principles** 🔑: **universal participation**, **political equality**, **majority rule** (50% + 1).
- **Representative democracy** — direct participation is impractical; representatives must show **responsiveness**.
- **Substantive theory:** policies must guarantee **civil liberties** (e.g. **S.10 — no state religion**) and **civil rights** (S.42).
- Democracy needs at minimum **universal suffrage, political parties, regular elections**.
- **Sargent (1990) — five elements** 🔑: citizen involvement in decision making; some equality; some liberty; a system of representation; an electoral system of majority rule.

### Good Governance
- **Akinseye-George (2000):** governance subject to **accountability, transparency and openness**, and **legitimate**.
- **IMF:** transparency, effective public resource management, a stable regulatory environment for the private sector.
- A purely technical (macroeconomic) notion is **dubious** without people's welfare.
- **Ten characteristics** 🔑: policies that bring happiness/good life; accountability; transparency; predictability; rational decisions; openness; free flow of information; **rule of law**; **press freedom**; **decentralisation**.
- **Social contract:** people formed the state to escape the state of nature where life was "**solitary, nasty, brutish and short**" (Hobbes); deviation = corruption.
- **Corruption** = the major obstacle. **Accountability procedure:** self-disclosure, scrutiny, ownership of decisions by the people, resources used for development, due process; the war on corruption is a **war on poverty**.

### Conclusion
- Law, democracy and good governance together guarantee **freedom, liberty and dividends of democracy**; government must fulfil the social contract and be **transparent and accountable**.

**Likely questions:** Define law (Austin); functions of law; law vs morality; classifications of law; characteristics of customary law (with cases); classifications of constitutions; amendment procedure (S.9(2)); 1979 transition stages; non-justiciability of Chapter II (S.6(6)(c)); list fundamental rights with sections; fair hearing maxims; *actus reus*/*mens rea*; defences; felony vs misdemeanour; recall vs impeachment; procedural vs substantive democracy; characteristics of good governance.

## CHAPTER 7 — Concept of Economics and Development (Samuel O. Orekoya)

### Origin and Subject Matter
- Economics grew out of **moral philosophy, law and religion**; it became a distinct discipline with **Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776)**. 🔑
- The consensus subject matter is **scarcity**: human **wants are unlimited**, **means/resources are limited** → **choice** → **opportunity cost**. 🔑
- Economic theory assumes **rationality** in decision making (affected by family, socio-cultural environment, peers).
- **"Means"** → production (creating goods and services); **"wants"** → consumption ("use-up" of goods).
- **Production questions:** what to produce, in what quantity, how (capital–labour mix), how to organise production.
- **Types of consumption:**
  - **Intermediate (for further production)** vs **final** (ends the good's existence).
  - **Durable** (cars, electronics) vs **non-durable** (food, daily needs).
  - **Necessity** (crucial to survival) vs **luxury** (avoidable without threat to survival).
- 🔑 **Law of demand:** inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded (higher price → lower quantity).
  - A change in **own price** → **movement along** the demand curve.
  - Changes in **prices of related goods (substitutes/complements), income, taxes, tastes** → **shift of** the demand curve.
- **Distribution and exchange** = the connecting cord between production and consumption, which are separated by:
  - **Location** (e.g. GSM recharge cards made in a few cities, used everywhere).
  - **Time** (e.g. seasonal farm produce eaten all year).
- **Adjusting to demand:**
  - **Temporary** rise (festive/dry season) → use installed capacity more (overtime).
  - **Permanent** rise (population growth) → **capital formation**, i.e. build new plants.
- Economists share a **scientific methodology** and an **opportunity-cost** lens. Scope has expanded to **institutions** and **environmental externalities** (climate change, global warming).
- **Applied fields:** agricultural, engineering, monetary, health, education, environmental, international and managerial economics.

### Definitions of Economics 🔑
| Scholar | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Adam Smith** | "An inquiry into the **nature and causes of the wealth of nations**" |
| **J.S. Mill** | "The **practical science of the production and distribution of wealth**" |
| **Alfred Marshall** | "The study of **mankind in the ordinary business of life**" |
| **Lionel Robbins** (most accepted) | "A social science which studies **human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses**" |
| Modern | How man allocates scarce resources with alternative uses to achieve given ends; "how limited resources are used to satisfy unlimited wants"; "how choices are made to cope with scarcity" |

- Economics deals with: creating wealth from scarce resources; production and distribution of goods and services; behaviour and welfare of the groups involved; **trade-offs**.

### Key Concepts
- **Science:** systematic study of how the world works. Process: observation of regularities → generalisations → **hypotheses** → testing against data.
- **Model:** a simplified representation of reality. 🔑
- **Positive economics** = "**what is**": testable, free of value judgements (true or false). 🔑
- **Normative economics** = "**what ought to be**": based on value judgements. 🔑
- **Social science / rationality:** human behaviour is erratic, so it is studied under ***ceteris paribus***. Individuals pick the option with the **greatest net gain**.
- **Economic person (*homo economicus*):** a theoretical abstraction of a person acting solely to maximise profit or benefit.
- **Economy:** the mechanism that organises scarce resources to produce goods and services.
- **Three economic agents** 🔑:
  - **Households** — consume goods and services, **own the factors of production**, and aim to **maximise utility**.
  - **Firms** — produce, buy factor services, and aim to **maximise profit**.
  - **Government** — sets the rules and laws, produces to some extent, and **redistributes** resources for welfare.
- **Four factors of production:** **land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship**. 🔑
- **Needs** = essential for life; **wants** = desired to improve quality of life. Wants are **insatiable**.
- **Means** = resources (time, money, material).
- **Scarcity** = resources are **limited relative to wants**, even when they are plentiful. This is not the everyday sense of "rarely found". It is the **basic economic problem**. 🔑
- **Choice** = forced by scarcity; shaped by **tastes/preferences** (not measurable) and **opportunities/constraints** (measurable).
- **Opportunity cost** = the value of the **next best alternative forgone**. It is **what is not chosen** (e.g. government builds roads instead of schools). 🔑
- **Scale of preference** = a list of wants **ranked from most to least pressing**. 🔑
- **Incentives** are the most significant force in economics.
- **Economic efficiency** = maximum benefit for a given cost, or minimum cost for a benefit. Two types:
  - **Productive efficiency** = maximum output from inputs, with no waste.
  - **Allocative efficiency** = resources used to produce the goods of **highest value**.
  - Together they answer the **what, how and for whom** questions.

### Branches 🔑
- **Microeconomics:** individuals, households, firms and markets; **partial** analysis (other conditions held fixed).
- **Macroeconomics:** the economy as a whole — total output, price level, growth, inflation, exchange rates, the **business cycle** (ups and downs of total output and employment).

### Economic Systems 🔑
1. **Capitalist (market economy):** free enterprise; the **price mechanism** allocates resources; minimal state role. Example: **USA**.
2. **Socialist (planned/command economy):** the state owns and controls the means of production; a **central planning authority** replaces the price mechanism. Example: the **defunct USSR**.
3. **Communist:** an **advanced stage of socialism**; means of production **owned by the masses**; no economic classes.
4. **Mixed:** both market and state; government steps in to correct **market failure**. Example: most countries, especially developing ones (**Nigeria, Ghana**).

### Economic Growth vs Development 🔑
- Growth is a **subset** of development. Development presupposes growth, but growth doesn't guarantee development.
- **Economic growth (Todaro 2015):** "a **steady process** by which the **productive capacity** of the economy is increased over time to bring about **rising levels of national income**."
- **Measures of growth:**
  - **GNP** = total domestic and foreign value added claimed by a country's **residents** (no deduction for depreciation).
  - **GDP** = total value of final output produced **within** an economy by residents and non-residents.
  - **GNP = GDP + net factor income from abroad.**
  - Economists prefer **per capita** values, because if population grows faster than output, per capita income falls.
- **Economic development:**
  - Traditional view: capacity to sustain annual growth of **5% or more**.
  - Redefined as the **reduction/elimination of poverty, inequality and unemployment** within a growing economy.
  - "A **multidimensional process** involving major changes in social structures, popular attitudes and national institutions, as well as accelerating growth, reducing inequality and eradicating poverty."
  - In short: the sustained elevation of society toward a "**better or more humane life**".

### Human Development Index (HDI) — UNDP 🔑
- An alternative to per capita income, focused on **enlarging people's choices**.
- **Three components:**
  1. **Life expectancy at birth**
  2. **Educational attainment** (mean years of schooling + adult literacy)
  3. **Standard of living** (per capita income)
- **Method:** separate ends from means → translate indicators to a common denominator → weight them into a composite index.

### Three Objectives of Development (Todaro) 🔑
1. Increase the availability and widen the distribution of **basic life-sustaining goods** (food, shelter, health, protection).
2. **Raise levels of living** (income, jobs, education, cultural values), which builds **self-esteem**.
3. **Expand economic and social choices**, freeing people from servitude, dependence, ignorance and misery.

### Characteristics of Developing Countries 🔑
1. **Low levels of living:** low incomes, high inequality, poor health, inadequate education.
   - **Absolute poverty** = below the minimum income needed for basic physical needs.
   - **Relative poverty** = income relative to the national average.
2. **Low capital accumulation** (low savings and investment) → low productivity.
3. **Low labour productivity**, from lack of complementary inputs (**law of diminishing marginal productivity**).
4. **High population growth and dependency burden.** The labour force is ages **15–64**; children and the elderly are dependants.
5. **High unemployment and underemployment.**
   - **Underemployment** = working below capacity, or so unproductive that cutting their hours barely affects output.
   - **Open unemployment** = able and willing to work, but no jobs.
6. **Dependence on agriculture and primary product exports** (cash crops, crude oil, raw materials); small share of world trade.

### Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) 🔑
- From the **UN Millennium Summit, September 2000**. **8 goals**, target year **2015**.
- Goals 1–7 are mutually reinforcing; goal 8 is the **means**.

- Officially "**Transforming our World**"; **17 goals, 169 targets**; **193 UN member states**; the post-2015 agenda; built on "**The Future We Want**"; target year **2030**.
- **Three dimensions** of sustainable development: **economic, social, environmental**.

## CHAPTER 8 — Growth and Development of the Nigerian Economy (S.O. Orekoya & N. Olaseinde)

### Background
- **Independence from Britain in 1960**; became a **republic in 1963**.
- **Natural resources:** crude oil, columbite, gold, silver, iron ore, marble, lead, zinc, tin, natural gas, limestone.
- At independence the economy was **underdeveloped**: low productivity, low per capita income, poor infrastructure, dominated by **agriculture** (primary sector).
- **Four National Development Plans (1962–1985)** → **SAP (1986)** → **rolling plans** (1990s) → democratic-era programmes (NEEDS, 7-Point Agenda, Transformation Agenda, ERGP).
- **Plan objectives (Anyanwu et al. 1997):** raise real income with equitable distribution; reduce unemployment; more Nigerian ownership of enterprises; use local resources; economic stability.
- **Decade by decade:**
  - **1960s:** political crises, military intervention, **civil war**.
  - **1970s:** oil boom, big state role in the economy.
  - **1980s:** **oil glut** → crisis → military return and SAP.
  - **1990s:** liberalisation and rolling plans.
  - **Since 1999:** democratic, "paradigm" approaches.

### Growth Definition
- **Simon Kuznets (1971):** growth is "the **long-term rise in capacity to supply increasingly diverse economic goods** to its population… based on **advancing technology** and the **institutional and ideological adjustments** it demands." 🔑
- Measured by GDP, GNP or GNI; **GDP growth rate** is the conventional measure.

### Structure and Performance (Tables 8.1 & 8.2) 🔑
- Main sectors: **agriculture; oil and mining; manufacturing; services**.
- **Real GDP growth:**
  - **Highest average: 12.6% (2001–05).**
  - **Lowest: −1% (1981–85)**, from austerity after the **oil glut**.
  - The first two decades grew on the **oil boom**: post-war rehabilitation and salary increases (the **Udoji awards**).
- **Agriculture:**
  - No appreciable growth 1960–80.
  - **All-time low of −1.5% (1976–80)**, caused by **Dutch disease** (oil boom pulled incentives away from agriculture).
  - Recovered to **3.6% and 7.0%** in the two halves of the 1980s; boomed at **18.1% (2001–05)**; then fell to **6.51% (2006–10)** and **4.11% (2011–15)**.
- **Inflation:** rose to **18.1% (1976–80)**; **peaked at 42% (1991–95)**; down to single digit **9.72% (2011–15)**. The **1980s** were a recession.
- **External sector:** in the 1990s exports grew up to ~**76%** and imports ~**82%**. Exports grew **−3.96% (2011–15)**, the lowest since 1986. Imports grew faster than exports → negative trade balance → push to **diversify** away from oil.
- **Exchange rate:**
  - The naira was **stronger than the dollar until 1985**.
  - **₦4.5/$1** in the late 1980s (SAP); **₦125.8/$1** in 2005–08; about **₦192.44/$1 by end-2015**.
- **External reserves:** rose from **$1.9b** (late 1970s) to **$43.7b** (2005–08); grew **−1.17%** in 2011–15.
- **Debt:** about **$32b** (2000–04). The **HIPC (Heavily Indebted Poor Countries) initiative** wrote off over half → **~$6.6b** (2005–08).
- **Sectoral share of real GDP:**
  - **Agriculture** was the largest contributor (**37–41%**, e.g. 41.07% in 1986–90) until 2006–15, when **services** took the lead (**32.3%** and **35.61%**). Agriculture fell to **24.94%** and **23.31%**.
  - **Oil and mining:** peaked at **28.6% (2001–05)**; **11.97%** in 2011–15.
  - **Manufacturing** is among the least: down from **9.17% (1981–85)** to **3.91% (2001–05)**, then up to **8.87% (2011–15)**.
  - **Building and construction:** the smallest, **1.77–3.58%**.

### Sectoral Analysis
**Agriculture**
- Employs about **65% of the labour force**; closely tied to the rural sector.
- **Policy objectives:** food and fibre self-sufficiency; better welfare; lower **food inflation**; diversify foreign exchange earnings through agricultural exports; raw materials for agro-industry.
- **1950s–60s:** agricultural development corporations and **farm settlement schemes**.
- Other tools: concessionary interest (loans capped at **6% in the 1970s, 9% in the 1980s**), specialised banks, commodity boards, input subsidies.
- **Programmes and institutions** 🔑:
  - **Operation Feed the Nation (1976)**
  - **Green Revolution (1980)**
  - **NACB – Nigerian Agricultural Co-operative Bank (1973)**
  - **ACGS – Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme (1977)**
  - River Basin Authorities, **NDE**, **DFRRI** (Directorate of Foods, Roads and Rural Infrastructure), National Agricultural Land Development Authority, **FEAP** (Family Economic Advancement Programme)
- Yet Nigeria still **imports rice** worth billions.

**Industrial sector** = manufacturing + oil, quarrying and mining + electricity.
- **Manufacturing:** under **10%** of GDP; capacity utilisation about **50%**. Causes: imported inputs, low quality, little export diversification.
- **Oil:**
  - Prominent since the **1970s boom**; the largest source of exports, foreign earnings and revenue.
  - **Proven reserves of ~32 billion barrels**; **6th largest producer in OPEC**; would last about **35 years** at the current rate. 🔑
  - **Nigeria LNG** exports gas to Europe, especially **Italy**.
  - Solid minerals are underdeveloped: coal, tin, kaolin, gypsum, columbite, gold, barite, marble, tantalite, salt, sulphur.
- **Electricity:**
  - Renamed **ECN (Electricity Corporation of Nigeria) → NEPA (National Electric Power Authority) → PHCN (Power Holding Company of Nigeria)**, then **unbundled** for privatisation. 🔑
  - Generation capacity raised to **over 20,000 MW** (hydro and gas).

**Federal core industrial projects** 🔑
- **Refineries:**
  - **Four**, all government-owned: **Warri, Kaduna, and two in Port Harcourt**.
  - About **20 private licences** since 2000, but none built.
  - Nigeria still imports **over two-thirds** of its petroleum products, hence the **subsidy** debate.
- **Fertiliser plants:** **FSFC** (Federal Super-Phosphate Fertiliser Co., **90%**) and **NAFCON** (National Fertiliser Co. of Nigeria, **10%**). They depend on imported inputs → shortages every planting season.
- **Steel:**
  - **Three steel plants:** **Itakpe, Ajaokuta, Aladja**.
  - **Three inland rolling mills:** **Osogbo, Jos, Katsina**.
  - All are a "**colossal failure**" and currently non-active.
- **Pulp and paper:**
  - **Nigeria Paper Mill, Jebba**; **Nigerian Newsprint Manufacturing Ltd**; **Iwopin Pulp and Paper Co. Ltd**.
  - Problems: poor funding, forex shortages, high costs, obsolete equipment.

### National Development Plans 🔑
| Plan | Objectives and key points | Why it failed / problems |
|---|---|---|
| **1st NDP (1962–68)** | Accelerate growth, raise living standards, reduce external dependence; priority on agriculture and industry. Projects: **Port Harcourt refinery, Kainji Dam, Nigerian Security Printing and Minting** | Insufficient funds (**50% expected from foreign aid** fell short); foreign advisers/inadequate manpower; federal–regional wrangling; **civil war** |
| **2nd NDP (1970–74)** | Post-war restructuring. **Five objectives:** united, strong, self-reliant nation; great dynamic economy; just and egalitarian society; land of bright and full opportunities; free and democratic society. First **oil windfall**. Reforms: **naira replaced the British pound**; **indigenisation decree**. Created River Basin Authorities, **ITF**, National Economic Advisory Council, National Bank for Agriculture, **SON**, **NNOC** | Inadequate **capacity to execute** |
| **3rd NDP (1975–80)** | Drafted in **oil boom** affluence. Added: higher per capita income, even income distribution, less unemployment, high-level manpower, **diversification**, balanced development, **indigenisation** | Overstated government capacity; negative oil shock; **external borrowing began** (start of rising debt) |
| **4th NDP (1981–85)** | Growth, price stability, social equity; priority on agriculture, education, manpower, infrastructure, housing, health | **Negative oil shocks** of the early 1980s; high cost of democratic government; **corruption**; inflation; debt; incoherent fiscal and monetary policy |

### Structural Adjustment Programme (SAP) 🔑
- Introduced in **July 1986** (the intro also says June 1986). Planned for **2 years (1986–88)** but lasted **until 1993**.
- **Context:** deep recession, structural imbalance, fiscal and exchange rate crises, balance-of-payments and debt problems.
- **Objectives:**
  - Restructure and **diversify** the economy away from oil and imports.
  - Fiscal and **balance-of-payments viability**.
  - Non-inflationary growth.
  - Reduce unproductive public investment and boost the **private sector**.
- **Policies:**
  - **Realistic exchange rate** and **trade liberalisation** (the lead policies)
  - **Deregulation** / market forces
  - **Removal of subsidies**
  - **Privatisation and commercialisation**, smaller public sector
  - Strong **demand management**
- **Effects:** more financial institutions; the most **controversial** policy package; caused **hardship, riots and protests**.

### Rolling Plans 🔑
- Shift from 5-year medium-term plans to **3-year rolling plans** in the early 1990s.
- **1990–92 (the first):** food and raw material self-sufficiency; self-reliant industry; jobs; socio-political awareness; market economy; cushion the effects of adjustment. **Failed:** relied on **deficit financing** and became a pile of uncompleted projects.
- **1993–96:** the first under the **National Planning Commission (NPC, Decree 12 of 1992)**. Addressed the naira, inflation, manufacturing and debt. **Problems:** excluded **local government estimates**; coincided with political crises and strikes.
- **Lessons:** government was the main agent (holding the "**commanding heights**"); the private sector was marginalised. Yet **over 70% of the labour force** is in the **informal (unorganised) sector**.

### Democratic-Era Programmes (since 1999) 🔑
**1. NEEDS (National Economic Empowerment and Development Strategy)**
- Focuses on **poverty reduction**, growth, human development and changing how business is done. A **paradigm shift** in planning.
- Emphasises policy environment over projects; **measurable targets**; **bottom-up**, participatory; keyed to the **MDGs**.
- Counterparts: **SEEDS** (states) and **LEEDS** (local governments).
- **Financing:** efficient use of resources, domestic savings, **FDI, ODA, debt relief**.
- **Outcomes:** more FDI and a successful **debt relief/payoff**.

**2. Seven-Point Agenda (released 1 August 2007, Yar'Adua)**
1. **Power and Energy** — to make Nigeria an industrial nation by 2015
2. **Food Security and Agriculture** — a 5–10 fold rise in yields
3. **Wealth Creation and Employment** — diversify away from oil
4. **Mass Transportation** — modernise the **railways**
5. **Land Reform** — release land for commercial farming
6. **Security**
7. **Qualitative and Functional Education**
- Failed mainly because of its **short lifespan**.

**3. Transformation Agenda (2011–2015, President Jonathan)**
- Aimed to fix the lack of long-term perspective and the lack of the **3Cs: continuity, consistency, commitment**.
- **Projected investment ₦40.75 trillion:** ₦24.45tn public (**60%**) and ₦16.30tn private (**40%**).
- **Five priorities:**
  1. Harmony between fiscal and monetary policy
  2. Sound macro policy with **single-digit inflation**
  3. Review of the budget process (clear executive–legislature roles, prompt appropriation)
  4. Review of the **revenue allocation formula** (balanced fiscal federalism)
  5. Institutionalise development planning at all levels
- **Critique:** the **absence of party ideology** leads to discontinuity. One party produced **three presidents** with three unrelated programmes.

**4. ERGP — Economic Recovery and Growth Plan (2017–2020, Buhari/Osinbajo)**
- Aims to restore growth after the **recession**. Builds on the Strategic Implementation Plan.
- Government's role shifts from "**omnibus provider**" to **removing bottlenecks** for innovation and markets. Leverages **STI** for a **knowledge-based economy**; aligned with the SDGs.
- **Three broad objectives:**
  1. **Restore growth** — macro stability (fiscal stimulus, monetary stability, external balance) and **diversification** (agriculture, energy, **MSME-led** industrialisation).
  2. **Invest in our people:**
     - **Social inclusion**, especially the **North East and Niger Delta**
     - **Jobs and youth empowerment** (**N-Power**, Made-in-Nigeria, local content; ICT and creative industries)
     - **Human capital** (health — roll out the **NHIS** nationwide; education; STEM)
  3. **Build a globally competitive economy:**
     - **Infrastructure** (power, roads, rail, ports, broadband; **PPP**)
     - **Business environment** (**PEBEC**; target **top 100** in the World Bank **Doing Business** index by 2020, **up from 169**)
     - **Digital-led growth** (**Smart Nigeria Digital Economy Project**, broadband, e-government, ICT clusters in SEZs)
- **Five key executive priorities:**
  1. Stabilise the macroeconomy
  2. Agriculture and food security
  3. **Energy sufficiency** (power and petroleum)
  4. Transport infrastructure
  5. Industrialisation through **SMEs**

### Poverty and Human Development 🔑
- **Poverty** = lack of the resources needed to access basic needs, not just income: also health, education and services.
- **Three measurement approaches:**
  1. **Relative poverty:**
     - Measured against the majority's living standard.
     - **Non-poor** = household per capita expenditure **above two-thirds** of the average; poor = below.
     - The poor are split into **core (extreme) poor** and **moderate poor**.
  2. **Absolute poverty** (the **Food Energy Intake** measure):
     - Minimum needed for food, clothing, healthcare and shelter.
     - The book counts as poor those who can't afford **40% of 3,000 calories/day**.
     - World Bank **PPP**: living on **under US$1 per day**.
  3. **Subjective poverty:** **self-assessment**, i.e. respondents' own opinion of whether they are poor.
- **Nigerian poverty (NBS 2012):** the poor rose from about **28% (1980) to 69% (2010)**, a ~**150% increase**.
  - In 2010: non-poor **31.0%**, moderate poor **30.3%**, **extreme poor 38.7%**.
  - In 1980: moderate poor 21.0%, extreme poor 6.2%.

## CHAPTER 10 — Psychobiological Basis of Behaviour and Methods of Measurement in Psychology (A.O. Taiwo, S.K. Balogun, S.A. Okhakume & O. Adeshina)

### Introduction
- Experience, behaviour and personal identity are **products of the brain**. "**Human behaviour comes from the brain.**" 🔑
- The psychobiological basis of behaviour = how the **brain and nervous system** generate behaviour.
- **Philosophical questions:** the mind–brain relationship; can a mind exist without a brain?
- **Practical questions:** can biology predict alcoholism, depression, schizophrenia? Can disorders like antisocial personality, insomnia, anxiety attacks and hyperkinetic disorders be prevented?

### Four Types of Psychobiological Explanation 🔑
1. **Physiological** — relates an activity to how the brain and organs function, down to the **cellular/chemical** level.
2. **Ontogenetic** — how a structure or behaviour **develops** (from the genes plus environmental influence).
3. **Evolutionary** — relates behaviour to the **evolutionary history of a species**.
4. **Functional** — why a behaviour evolved / what it is for.

- The aim of **biological psychology** is to study the bodily mechanisms that make behaviour possible, and to improve health and well-being.
- **Two types of explanation:**
  - Factors that **force** behaviour (instincts/reflexes): sweating when hot, **pupil constriction** in bright light, wet palms when anxious, the **knee-jerk**, salivating at unsweetened lemon juice.
  - Factors that **enable** behaviour.
- **Two communication systems** in the body: the **endocrine** and **nervous** systems. 🔑

### The Endocrine System 🔑
- Glands release chemicals called **hormones**. Each hormone affects only cells with **receptor sites** for it.

| Gland | Hormone(s) | Function / effect of imbalance |
|---|---|---|
| **Thyroid** | Thyroxine / triiodothyronine | Raises **metabolic rate**, growth and maturation. Over-secretion → **hyperactivity**; under-secretion → **low metabolism** |
| **Parathyroid** | **Parathormone** | Raises **blood calcium**, lowers potassium. Under-secretion → poor bone formation; over-secretion → **lethargy** |
| **Adrenal medulla** | **Epinephrine and norepinephrine** | Prepares the body for **stress/emergency** (faster heartbeat, higher blood pressure, constricts gut blood vessels) |
| **Adrenal cortex** | Aldosterone, cortisol, corticosterone | Sodium metabolism, food metabolism, sexual maturity/activity |
| **Pancreas** | **Insulin / glucagon** | Lowers **blood sugar**. Over-secretion → lethargy; under-secretion → **diabetes** |
| **Anterior pituitary** ("**master gland**") | **Growth hormone (somatotropic)**, thyrotropic, adrenocorticotropic, FSH, LH, **prolactin** | Growth: excess → **pituitary giant**; deficiency → **pituitary dwarfism**. Controls the thyroid, adrenal cortex and gonads. Prolactin → **mammary glands**. Synthesises **six hormones**, released under the hypothalamus's control |
| **Posterior pituitary** | **Oxytocin**, **vasopressin** (antidiuretic hormone) | An extension of the **hypothalamus**; releases **two hormones**. Oxytocin → uterine contraction in **childbirth** and **milk release**. Vasopressin → constricts blood vessels, raises blood pressure |
| **Ovaries** (female) | **Estrogen, progesterone** | Female sex characteristics |
| **Testes** (male) | **Androgens, testosterone** | Male secondary sexual characteristics (facial hair), sexual behaviour |
| **Pineal** | Melatonin (book: "melanin") | Affects the gonads/suppresses hormone output; under-secretion → decreased sexual vigour |
| **Thymus** | **Thymosin** | Supports **immune responses** |

### The Nervous System 🔑
- A network of specialised cells (**neurons**) that coordinate actions and transmit signals.
- **Central Nervous System (CNS)** = **brain + spinal cord**.
- **Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)** = all nerves **outside** the brain and spinal cord.
- The **brain** is the control centre for all behaviour. The human brain is larger than expected for body size among primates (**Herculano-Houzel 2009**).

### Divisions of the Brain 🔑
| Division | Structures | Functions |
|---|---|---|
| **Forebrain** (largest in mammals) | **Cerebral cortex**; sub-cortical: **thalamus, basal ganglia, limbic system** | Receives **sensation**; initiates **movement**; **cognition** (thinking, talking, creating, imagining) |
| **Midbrain** | Tectum, **superior and inferior colliculus**, tegmentum, **substantia nigra** (linked to **Parkinson's disease**) | Connects the other two divisions; **alerts the forebrain** to incoming sensations |
| **Hindbrain** (most posterior) | **Medulla, pons, cerebellum** | **Sleeping and waking**; **coordination of movement**; **vital reflexes** (heart rate, blood pressure, respiration) |

### The Cortex and Lobes 🔑
- **Cortex** = a thin layer of cells covering the forebrain; holds most of its neurons.
- **Frontal lobe** (front):
  - Social-emotional behaviour and **personality** (injury → personality change).
  - **Planning** and initiating **voluntary movement** (injury → **paralysis**).
- **Parietal lobe** (behind the frontal): **body sensations** (touch, pain, temperature) from the skin and joints.
- **Temporal lobe** (below the parietal): **hearing and speech**.
- **Occipital lobe** (very back): **vision**.

### Hemispheres 🔑
- **Left hemisphere:** **verbal, mathematical, analytic**.
- **Right hemisphere:** **non-verbal** (can read and understand simply but **cannot speak**), **spatial, holistic**.

### Brain Cells 🔑
- **Two types:** **neurons** and **glial cells**.
- About **100 billion neurons**; glial cells are **10–50 times more** numerous.
- **Neuron facts:**
  - **Dendrites** carry signals **to** the cell body; **axons** carry signals **away**.
  - Signals travel from a fraction of an inch to **over 3 feet**, at about **200 miles per hour**.
  - Humans grow **virtually no new neurons after birth**, but **Princeton** researchers found **adult monkeys** keep adding new neurons.
- **Neuron structure:**
  1. **Dendrites** — branch-like; **receive** signals from other neurons, muscles and sense organs.
  2. **Cell body** — egg-shaped; provides fuel, makes chemicals, maintains the neuron. Its **nucleus** holds the **DNA**.
  3. **Axon** — a single thread-like extension; carries signals **away** to a neighbouring neuron, organ or muscle.
  4. **Terminal buttons** — bulb-like swellings at the axon tips; their **vesicles** store **neurotransmitters**. They don't physically touch the next cell.
  5. **Synapse** — the tiny gap between a terminal button and the next cell.
  6. **Neurotransmitters** — chemicals released into the synapse that **excite or inhibit** the next cell.
- **Signal pathway** 🔑: **Dendrites → Cell body → Axon → Terminal buttons → Neurotransmitter → next neuron/muscle/organ.**
- **Glial cells** ("supporting cells"; they lack an **action potential**):
  - Provide **scaffolding** for developing neurons.
  - Wrap around neurons as **insulation** against interfering signals.
  - Release chemicals that influence neuron **growth and function**.
- Nervous integration comes from the nervous system **with the help of the endocrine system**.

### Measurement in Psychology
- **Psychological measurement** = using specified testing procedures to evaluate people's **abilities, behaviours and personal qualities**, i.e. how they differ from or resemble others.
- **Thorndike and Hagen (1986):** measurement is "**the process of quantifying observations (or descriptions) of a quality or attribute of a thing or person**." 🔑
- **Three steps of measurement** 🔑:
  1. **Identify and define** the attribute.
  2. Determine **operations** that make it **manifest/perceivable**.
  3. Establish procedures for **translating observations into quantitative statements**.
- **Variable** = anything that varies and can be measured (e.g. sex, socio-economic status).

### Types of Variables 🔑
- **Independent variable (IV)** — **manipulated** by the experimenter; the "**cause**"; stands alone. Examples: price, deprivation, temperature, noise, brain lesions.
- **Dependent variable (DV)** — changes as a function of the IV; the "**effect**".
- **Example:** "Effect of price on consumer buying behaviour": **price = IV**, **quantity purchased = DV**.
- **Experimental group** = exposed to the IV. **Control group** = all conditions **except** the IV (Shenge and Icheke 2005).

### Types of Measurement
1. **Score/numerical** — assigns a number (e.g. GES 103 scores: Ayo 60, Olabisi 55, Mojisola 65, Banke 45, Dare 56).
2. **Nominal/category** — decides which category a case belongs to (e.g. lorry driver, security officer, cashier). The numbers are **frequencies (counts)**, not scores.

### Four Scales of Measurement 🔑 (very common exam question)
| Scale | Key property | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominal** | **Names/labels** only; the **lowest** form; numbers have no meaning (Balogun 2005) | SES: (1) high, (2) medium, (3) low as labels; sex |
| **Ordinal** | **Rank order** only (smallest → largest); also called "**rank measurement**" | 1st, 2nd, 3rd |
| **Interval** | Rank + **equal distances** between values; the **most widely used** in research | 4, 6, 8, 10 (gap from 4→6 = gap from 8→10) |
| **Ratio** | Interval + an **absolute (true) zero** | Distance, weight, tape measure |

### Methods of Data Collection 🔑
**A. Observational method** — systematic observation is the **starting point of any science**.
1. **Case study** — studies one individual **in great depth**; used in clinical settings to generate hypotheses.
   - 🔑 **Paul Broca (1824–1880)** studied the patient "**Tan**", who could only say "tan".
   - Broca hypothesised the **speech production centre** is in the **frontal lobe of the left hemisphere**; the **autopsy confirmed** it.
2. **Survey** — asks about behaviour, opinions, attitudes, feelings, knowledge.
   - Via **interviews** (face-to-face) or **questionnaires** (structured/unstructured, open/closed-ended).
   - Shows how strong or widespread a reaction or problem is.
3. **Naturalistic observation** — observing natural behaviour **without interfering** (e.g. watching children play through a **one-way glass**). Useful in **early stages** of research.

**B. Correlational method** 🔑
- **No manipulation** of the IV; assesses relationships among **naturally occurring variables** to find **predictive relationships** (used in diagnosis and job selection).
- **Correlation** = two measures **covary**.
  - **Positive:** both rise together (e.g. **smoking ↔ lung disease**).
  - **Negative:** one rises as the other falls (e.g. **more TV time → lower achievement test scores**, Keith, Reimers et al. 1986).
- The **correlation coefficient** gives **direction and strength**.
- **Correlation does not prove causation.**

**C. Experimental method** 🔑
- A **controlled experiment**: observations under **systematically varied conditions**. The investigator **manipulates** the IV and observes the effect on the DV.
- Uses **experimental vs control groups**.
- Used to **test hypotheses** and establish **cause-and-effect**. It arose because naturalistic observation can't establish causality.

### Why Psychological Measurement? 🔑
1. Formalises **prediction** of behaviour with minimum error and maximum accuracy.
2. Makes sense of **individual differences** and how traits form a unique **personality**.
3. Helps learn **how people develop** (at what age skills appear; role of sex, race, intelligence).

- **Instruments:** interviews, psychological tests, scales, inventories (intelligence, personality, creativity), **self-reports (self-inventories)**, judges' ratings.

### Requirements of a Good Test 🔑
- **Reliable** — gives **consistent** results over and over.
- **Valid** — measures **what it is intended to measure**.
- **Standardised** — administered to everyone **in the same way under the same conditions**, with **norms** for comparing scores.

### Standard Psychological Tests 🔑 (dates as given in the textbook)
| Test | Developer / date | Measures |
|---|---|---|
| **Stanford-Binet Intelligence Test** | 1960 (revision) | **Intelligence** |
| **MMPI** (Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory) | **Starke Hathaway & J.R. McKinley**, University of Minnesota (book says 1930) | **Personality** |
| **Rorschach Inkblot Test** | **Hermann Rorschach** (Swiss psychiatrist), **1924** in the book (published 1921) | **Personality** |
| **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)** | **Henry Murray** (American), **1938** | **Personality** |

### Statistical Techniques 🔑
**1. Descriptive statistics** — process raw data to **describe** a phenomenon:
- **Frequency distribution** — how often each score occurs; the first step is **ranking/ordering** scores by magnitude.
- **Central tendency:**
  - **Mode** = the most frequent score.
  - **Median** = the central score.
  - **Mean** = the "average".
- **Variability:**
  - **Range** = highest − lowest (the simplest measure).
  - **Standard deviation** = the average difference of scores from the mean.
- **Correlation coefficient** — the degree of relationship between two variables.

**2. Inferential statistics** — uses logic to **infer** from sample data to a population; assesses the **probability** of sample results (e.g. for policy decisions).

## CHAPTER 12 — Natural Resource Use, Exploitation and Sustainable Development (Yingigba C. Akinyemi)

### Definition
- **Resource** = any part of the environment (land, water, air, minerals, forest, wildlife) created by **natural processes** with the potential to **create wealth or satisfy human needs**; "the stock that can be drawn from nature for supporting life". 🔑
- Resource use is defined by a society's **culture, level of technology and economic system**.
- Rising demand → **depletion** and environmental problems; current use determines **future availability**.
- The link between resource use, environment, poverty and **intergenerational fairness** produced the idea of **sustainable development**, mostly from the **1987 Brundtland Report**. 🔑

### Classification of Resources 🔑
| Basis | Types |
|---|---|
| **Abundance/availability** | **Renewable (flow)** — replenish quickly; inexhaustible (water, forests, air, solar). **Non-renewable (stock)** — finite, cannot be renewed (coal, petroleum, minerals) |
| **Origin** | **Biotic** — living, from the biosphere (forests, plants, animals). **Abiotic** — non-living, inorganic (air, land, water, minerals) |
| **Geographic concentration** | **Diffuse** — cover large areas (forests). **Point** — highly concentrated, small area (minerals) |
| **Stage of development** | **Potential** — known to exist but unused (e.g. possible petroleum in **Lake Chad**). **Actual** — surveyed and in use (e.g. **Niger Delta** oil and gas). **Reserve** — the part extractable with available technology. **Stock** — the part not profitably developable for lack of technology |

**de Zeeuw (2001) — by time needed for renewal/recycling** 🔑
1. **Non-renewable, non-recyclable** — fossil fuels
2. **Non-renewable but recyclable** — minerals
3. **Quickly renewable** — fish
4. **Slowly renewable** — forests
5. **Environmental renewable** — air, water, soil (not depleted but can be **degraded by pollution**)
6. **Flow/continuous** — sunlight, wind (not reduced by use)

### Resource vs Resistance; Culture 🔑
- **Resistance** = a socio-cultural or physical **obstacle** that hinders the use of a resource: culture, technology, physical barriers.
- **Culture** = beliefs (religious, political), institutions (legal, educational, government) and **technology** (equipment and skills), i.e. the **total way of life** of a people.
- Traditions, religion and **taboos** shape perception of the environment.
- **Primitive societies** with low technology have limited ability to use resources.

### Types of Natural Resources
**1. Mineral resources** 🔑
- Elements, compounds, minerals or rocks concentrated enough to extract; **finite and non-renewable**; unevenly distributed.
- The earth has **92 naturally occurring elements**, but the crust is mostly **eight**: **oxygen, silicon, aluminium, iron, calcium, sodium, magnesium, potassium**.
- The ocean floor has **manganese oxide nodules** (manganese, iron, copper, nickel, cobalt).
- **Metallic minerals** (in ores with impurities): copper, lead, silicon, tin, aluminium, iron.
- **Non-metallic minerals:** nitrate, sulphur, slate, building stone, graphite, quartz, coal, petroleum.
- Stages: discovered → mined → transported → refined → manufactured.

**2. Energy resources** 🔑
- **Energy consumption is an index of economic development.**
- **Renewable:** solar, wind, hydro-electricity, tides, waves, **biomass** (organic matter: crops, plants, animal waste).
- **Biogas:** gases produced by **anaerobic micro-organisms** acting on domestic/agricultural waste in **digesters**. Mainly **methane**, plus CO₂, nitrogen, hydrogen and oxygen.
- **Non-renewable:** fossil fuels (crude oil, natural gas, coal, oil shales, tar sands); **nuclear** (**uranium, thorium**).
- **Geothermal:** heat from the earth's interior in **volcanic areas**; used in the **USSR, New Zealand, Iceland, Mexico, California**.

**3. Forest resources** 🔑
- Forests cover **32%** of the world's land: ~**2.5 billion ha** of closed forest and **1.2 billion ha** of open woodland and savannah.
- **Wood products:** pulp, building material, firewood.
- **Non-wood products:** bark dyes, fibres, gums, incense, latex, oils, tannins, fruits, nuts.
- **Ecosystem services:** control of water runoff, climate regulation, soil protection, water filtration, **carbon sequestration**.
- Threats: housing, urbanisation, cattle ranching.

**4. Water resources** 🔑
- Essential to life: dissolves nutrients, regulates body temperature, removes waste (Cunningham and Saigo 1999).
- Covers **over 70%** of the earth's surface. Total water is **over 1,404 million km³**: **97% in oceans**, **2% in polar ice**, and **1%** other (mostly **groundwater**).
- Freshwater comes from **surface water** (lakes, rivers) and **groundwater**.
- The global supply is **fixed and finite**.
- **Causes of shortage:**
  - **Natural:** low rainfall, hot winds, rivers changing course.
  - **Human:** rising demand, urbanisation, overgrazing, poor farming, inadequate sewage.

**5. Biodiversity** 🔑
- **Biodiversity** = the degree of variation of life; the number and abundance of species in a region.
- **Elements:**
  - **Genetic** diversity (gene variety within a species)
  - **Species** diversity (number of kinds in an ecosystem)
  - **Ecological/ecosystem** diversity (richness of a community: niches, trophic levels)
  - **Molecular** diversity
- Diversity **increases from the poles towards the tropics**. **Tropical moist forests hold 50%** of the world's species; North America and Europe only **10–15%**.
- **Functions:**
  - Food
  - Wood and industrial materials (fibre, oil, dyes, rubber, timber, paper)
  - **Traditional medicine**
  - **Modern medicine** (antibiotics from fungi/microbes; marine-derived drugs)
  - **Recreation** (bird watching, fishing, trekking)
  - **Indirect benefits:** carbon fixation, pollination, water cycles, groundwater recharge, soil formation, nutrient cycles, absorbing pollutants, climate regulation, aesthetic and cultural value (Rana 2007)

**6. Soil resources** 🔑
- To the geographer, soil is "**a living body formed by the interaction of certain forces and the work of certain processes**" (**Faniran and Ojo 1980**).
- Made of rock and mineral particles, water, air, plants, animals and their remains.
- Forms **extremely slowly** (thousands of years for a few cm), so although **renewable** it is effectively **irreplaceable**.
- World land area is **over 13 billion ha**; **less than half** is usable for agriculture; only **~1.4 billion ha** is currently suitable for crops.

### Characteristics of Resources 🔑
1. **Utility** — satisfy human needs through goods and services.
2. **Scarcity** — physical, **geopolitical** (trade barriers), economic (supply chain, market imperfections), quality.
3. **Depletion** — exhaustion of raw materials in a region; renewable resources are depleted when **extraction exceeds the renewal rate**.

### Depletion and Environmental Problems
- Resources also provide a **sink function** (absorbing and recycling waste).
- Over-demand plus technology → greenhouse gases, **deforestation**, biodiversity loss, land degradation, water and air pollution → **climate change, ozone depletion, sea-level rise, famine**.

**Soil degradation and erosion** 🔑
- **Causes:**
  - **Accelerated erosion** (water, wind) — the **major** form
  - **Salinisation** from waterlogging
  - Chemical and industrial pollution
  - Loss of nutrients (**N, P, K**) and organic matter
  - Non-farm use (reservoirs, highways, airports, parking)
- **Erosion** = washing or blowing away of surface soil. It is natural and slow; human removal of vegetation accelerates it, especially on **steep/long slopes**.
- **Rates:** **0.001 t/ha/yr** on flat vegetated land; **1–5 t/ha/yr** in mountains. About **75 billion tons** of fertile soil are lost each year (**Pimentel and Burgess 2013**).
- **Effects of erosion:**
  - Lower **crop yield** and higher cost
  - Less **water-holding** capacity
  - **Nutrient loss**, so fertiliser is needed
  - **Water pollution/siltation** (costly dredging)

**Deforestation** 🔑
- The **permanent destruction of indigenous forests**.
- World forest fell from **4,128 to 3,999 million ha (1990–2015, FAO 2015)**.
- **Causes:** timber harvest, agriculture, roads, firewood.
- **Effects:** species loss, **CO₂ release**, drier climate, flooding, **siltation**, poorer water quality, **desertification**.

**Biodiversity loss** 🔑
- **WWF 2014 (since 1970):** **76% of freshwater**, **39% of marine** and **39% of terrestrial** wildlife lost.
- **IUCN:**
  - **One-third** of known species threatened with extinction
  - **75%** of crop genetic diversity lost
  - **75%** of fisheries fully or over-exploited
  - **70%** of species at risk if temperature rises **over 3.5°C**
  - **One-third** of reef-building corals threatened
  - **Over 350 million** people face severe water scarcity
- **By income group:** **+10%** in high-income countries, **−18%** in middle-income, **−58%** in low-income.
  - Rich countries consume more than nature can replenish: their **ecological footprint** exceeds **biocapacity**.
- **Causes:**
  - **Habitat destruction**
  - **Climate change**
  - **Invasive species** (once natural barriers are destroyed)
  - **Pollution**, including **genetic pollution** (uncontrolled hybridisation, **gene swamping**)
  - **Overpopulation**
  - **Over-exploitation** (fishing, hunting, logging, illegal wildlife trade)
- **Freshwater ecosystems are the most threatened.** They cover **15%** of continental surface.
  - Since 1970, land species fell **25%** and freshwater species **55%**.
  - Dams, barrages and dykes block fish migration.

**Climate change** 🔑
- **IPCC (1995):** climate has changed significantly over the past century, **mainly due to human activities**.
- Projected global temperature rise of **1–3.5°C (2–6°F) by AD 2100**.
- **Greenhouse gases:** **CO₂, methane, CFCs, nitrous oxide**, from fossil fuels, cement, biomass burning, landfills, pipeline leaks and soil denitrification. They **absorb infrared radiation**.
- **Effects:**
  - Floods and droughts; biodiversity loss
  - Water scarcity; falling food production in the tropics
  - **Sea-level rise** and storm damage
  - **Spread of insect-borne diseases**; heat stress; weakened immune systems from UV
  - **Coral reef damage**

### Sustainability 🔑
- **Sustainability** = improving the quality of human life **within the limits of the global environment**, without degrading it or harming others.
- **Brundtland Commission, *Our Common Future* (1987):** "**the ability to meet the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.**" 🔑
- **Three basic concepts** (Mensah and Castro 2004):
  1. Living within the earth's **limits**.
  2. Understanding the links among **economy, society and environment**.
  3. **Fair distribution** of resources across present and future generations.
- **Three perspectives/pillars:**
  - **Environmental** — harvest, pollution and depletion rates that can continue **indefinitely** (Melville 2010).
  - **Economic** — support a defined level of production **indefinitely** (Anand and Sen 2000).
  - **Social** — a social system functioning at a defined level of well-being indefinitely (Hutchins and Sutherland 2008).
- **Munasinghe (1993)** model — balance the three:
  - **Economic:** growth, efficiency, stability
  - **Social:** poverty, equity, participation, empowerment, culture/heritage
  - **Environmental:** biodiversity/resilience, natural resources, pollution
- In business this is the **Triple Bottom Line**: **profit, planet, people**. 🔑

### Measuring Sustainability 🔑
- Integrate three areas: **technical/economic** (jobs, goods), **ecological footprint** (protecting resources), and **social** (environmental justice, participation).
- **Indicators:** **drivers** (processes) and **outcomes** (results).
- **Approaches:**
  1. **Capital approach** (UNECE 2009):
     - Balances **financial, natural, produced, human and social capital**.
     - Tracked through the **System of Integrated Environmental and Economic Accounts**.
     - **Weak sustainability:** natural and man-made capital are **substitutable**. Linked to the **Environmental Kuznets Curve**: the environment worsens in early growth, then improves past a **turning point** in per capita income.
     - **Strong sustainability:** the two are **complements, not substitutes**, so **critical environmental levels must be kept**.
  2. **Planetary boundaries** — **nine earth processes** with **tipping points**, beyond which change is uncontrollable:
     1. Climate change
     2. Ocean acidification
     3. Stratospheric ozone depletion
     4. Atmospheric aerosol loading
     5. Biochemical flows (**P and N cycles**)
     6. Global freshwater use
     7. Land-system change
     8. Biodiversity loss
     9. Chemical pollution
  3. **Ecological footprint** — the impact of human consumption and waste, expressed as the land area needed, in "**global hectares**" (Global Footprint Network 2010). Shows trade-offs between **present and future** generations and between **brown and green** agendas.

### Resource Use and Sustainable Development 🔑
- Development used to be seen narrowly as **economic growth** (capital formation from savings and investment).
- **Human development** puts **people at the centre** and enlarges their **choices**: education, health, income, decent living, freedom of expression/association/movement, social justice, non-discrimination, participation (National Human Development Report 2016). It aims to eradicate poverty and deprivation.
- **Sustainable development** = development meeting present needs without compromising the future. It must be:
  - **Socially desirable**
  - **Economically viable** (costs ≤ income)
  - **Ecologically sustainable**
- **What it involves:**
  - Using renewables without degrading them (recycling; **solar and geothermal** energy).
  - Optimal use and **equitable distribution** (Knox, Agnew and McCarthy 2008).
  - Education and health that build environmental awareness.
  - A shift from **globalisation to "localisation"**.
- **SDGs adopted 25 September 2015:** **17 goals, 169 targets**, over the next **15 years**; builds on the MDGs.
- **Technology** can be a threat (resource-using) or a solution (clean, resource-saving). Developed countries often prioritise **profit over environmental policy**.
- **Obstacles to sustainable development** 🔑:
  1. Heavy reliance on **fossil fuels** (inequality, global warming); renewables pursued "**half-heartedly**" because of powerful fossil-fuel interests.
  2. **Population growth** in less developed countries (hunger, malnutrition).
  3. **Inadequate institutional frameworks**.

### Revision Points (from the chapter)
- A resource is part of the environment used to satisfy human needs.
- Classified by **abundance, origin, geographic concentration, stage of development**.
- Sustainability has **social/political, environmental and economic** perspectives.
- Environmental problems of unsustainable use: **deforestation, biodiversity loss, global warming, climate change**.

## CHAPTER 13 — Current Discourse in Peace and Conflict Studies (A.L. Alabi, T.A. Joshua & Ojuge Folorunsho)

### Introduction
- Peace and conflict studies is **interdisciplinary**: sociology, political science, philosophy, history, psychology, social anthropology, geography, economics and religious studies. 🔑

### Origins and Development 🔑
- The field was born **simultaneously but separately** as two strands:
  - **"Peace research"** — focused on the **prevention of conflicts and maintenance of peace**.
  - **"Conflict studies"** — focused on **settling disputes and sustaining peace**.

**Peace research**
- Origins (**Robben and Ferrandiz 2007**): the moral reflections of **Woodrow Wilson, Georges Clemenceau and David Lloyd George** on the costs of **World War I**, where about **8 million soldiers died** and **2 million went missing**.
- This led to the **League of Nations (1919)**.
- Peace research developed in **Scandinavian universities in the 1950s**.
- In the **1950s** came calls for the systematic study of peace as a **separate discipline** (Gleditsch et al. 2014), distinct from **war studies**, which military scholars dominated.
- It has since broadened to human rights, **human security**, **transitional justice**, violence prevention, peace building, reconciliation, identity formation and cultural trauma.

**Conflict studies**
- Started in **North America in the 1950s–60s**, at the height of the **Cold War** and the **nuclear arms race**.
- **1957:** *Journal of Conflict Resolution* founded.
- **1959:** **Centre for Conflict Resolution, University of Michigan**.
- Treated conflict as a **general phenomenon** (international, domestic, industrial, community, family, interpersonal), borrowing from **industrial relations** and **community mediation**.
- **By the 1980s** it was affecting real conflicts:
  - **South Africa** — the **Centre for Intergroup Studies** worked on the apartheid confrontation.
  - **Middle East** — Israel–Arab negotiators trained through **problem-solving workshops**.
  - **Africa and South-East Asia** — humanitarian and development agencies built conflict resolution into their work.

**Merger of ideas (after the Cold War)**
- After the **dissolution of the Soviet Union**, attention shifted to **internal conflicts**: ethnic wars, secessionist movements, power struggles. These brought **mercenaries** and militias and diverted aid.
- This led to cross-fertilisation between peace research and conflict studies.
- **International bodies** set up peace building and conflict resolution mechanisms: **OSCE**, **OAU**, NGOs (**Jimmy Carter Centre**, **Nyerere Foundation**), the **IMF and World Bank**.
- **Preventing violent conflict** became a central goal of the new millennium.

### The Concept of Conflict 🔑
- Conflict is a **universal feature of human society**. It comes from economic differentiation, social change, cultural formation, psychological development and political organisation.
- **Lewis Coser (1956):** conflict is "**the struggle over values or claims to status, power and scarce resources, in which the aims of the groups or individuals are to neutralise, injure or eliminate rivals**." 🔑
  - It needs **two or more parties** with **incompatible interests or goals** (real or perceived) who show **hostile attitudes or damaging actions**.
- **Sources of conflicting interests** (Payson Conflict Group 2001):
  - Poor access to and distribution of resources (territory, money, energy, food)
  - Lack of control over political power and participation
  - **Loss of identity**
  - **Values** (government, religion, ideology)
- Conflicts are **dynamic** (they escalate and de-escalate). **Third parties** may become parties to the conflict.

### Types of Conflict 🔑
- **General types:** pseudo conflict, conflict of interests, value conflict, goal conflict, conflict of perception, role conflict, resource conflict.
- **Wallensteen (2002) — three types in international relations:**
  1. **Inter-state conflict** — between nation-states.
  2. **Internal conflict** — civil crises, ethnic wars.
  3. **State-formation conflict** — a variant of internal conflict: anti-colonial struggles, secession and autonomy movements, territorial disputes, battles for control of government.
- Other classifications of internal conflict: **intra-group, inter-group, national**.
- **Global conflicts:** non-state groups versus international and regional organisations.

### Theories on the Causes of Conflict (McCandless 2007) 🔑
**1. Social and psychological matters (agency)** — causes lie in individual or collective human behaviour; perceptions and misperceptions:
- Aggression is **innate/biologically programmed**.
- The **"self" vs "other"** distinction creates a psychological **need for enemies** (the **psychoanalytical** perspective).
- **Group formation**: images, misperceptions, **stereotyping, dehumanisation** (the **psycho-social** perspective).
- In the 1990s, "**ethnic conflict**" and "**identity conflict**" became common labels for African conflicts.

**2. Structural and political economy** — society's own organisation creates conflict:
- **Johan Galtung's "structural violence"**: unequal social structures produce **unequal access to resources**. 🔑
- **International political economy** and **war economy** literature: global processes cause systemic conflict; asks **who benefits from war** (Duffield, Keen, Goodhand and Hulme).

### Concepts Balancing the Two Perspectives 🔑
**(a) Protracted Social Conflict (PSC) — Edward Azar**
- Rooted in **religious, cultural or ethnic communal identity**.
- Caused by **dissatisfied basic developmental needs**: security, communal recognition, distributive justice.
- **Four causes:**
  1. **Communal content** — politicised identity groups.
  2. **Deprivation of human needs** — physical security, access to institutions, acceptance of identity.
  3. **The state and governance** — whether needs are met or denied.
  4. **International linkages** — economic dependency; **patron–client** military relations.
- Common in **developing countries** with extreme poverty. Rapid growth-oriented strategies can worsen inequality (**Azar 1990**).

**(b) Complex Political Emergencies (CPE) — Jonathan Goodhand and David Hulme**
- **Five features:**
  1. Conflicts **within and across state boundaries** (a hybrid form).
  2. **Political origins** — competition for power and scarce resources.
  3. **Protracted** duration.
  4. **Social cleavages** across political, economic, social and cultural lines.
  5. **Predatory social formations**, often **ethno-nationalist**, manipulated by **conflict entrepreneurs** and political opportunists.

### The Concept of Peace 🔑
- **Negative peace** = **absence of war/conflict**. It is no different from "non-war", a tense "**yet-to-be-violent**" situation (Miller 2005); the mere absence of violence despite antagonistic goals (**Galtung 1990**).
- **Positive peace** = a political condition ensuring **justice and social stability** through formal and informal institutions, practices and norms.
- **Four concepts of peace** (Payson Conflict Group 2001):
  1. Peace as **harmony** (absence of conflict)
  2. Peace as **order** (stability, peace through strength)
  3. Peace as **justice** (absence of domination and poverty)
  4. Peace as **conflict management** (a **process**, not an end)
- **Six conditions for positive peace** (Miller 2005):
  1. **Balance of political power** among groups
  2. **Legitimacy** of decision makers (transparency, accountability)
  3. Recognised **inter-dependent relationships** and cooperation
  4. **Reliable, trusted institutions** for resolving conflict
  5. A sense of **equality and respect**
  6. **Mutual understanding** of rights, interests and intents
- An **absence of conflict is impossible** in human society; peace is "notoriously elusive".

### Key Terms in Peace Studies 🔑
- **Peace building** — measures to **consolidate peaceful relations** and prevent tensions; promotes **human security**; strengthens society's capacity to manage conflict **without violence**.
- **Peace enforcement** — **military force** or its threat, usually under international authorisation (**Chapter VII of the UN Charter**), e.g. **Korea 1950–53**.
  - Can happen **without the consent** of the warring parties.
  - Includes sanctions, arms embargoes, air/missile strikes, rapid deployment, disarming armed groups.
- **Peace operations** — the umbrella term:
  - **Diplomatic** activities: preventive diplomacy, peacemaking, peace building.
  - **Military** activities: peacekeeping and peace enforcement.
- **Peacekeeping operations** — resolve or prevent escalation, halt fighting, uphold order, humanitarian action, restore institutions. Distinctive feature: conducted under a **UN or regional organisation mandate**.

### Key Terms in Conflict Resolution 🔑
- **Conflict resolution** — **transforms conflicts in an enduring manner** rather than just settling or suppressing them.
  - Addresses **basic human needs** and builds sustainable relationships (equality, multiculturalism, federalism).
  - Increases cooperation, reconciliation, institution building; prevents re-escalation.
- **PCIAS (Peace and Conflict Impact Assessment System)** — estimates the negative effects of escalation; **early warning** should also show the cost of a "wait-and-see" policy.
- **Conflict mitigation/management** — **contain and reduce violence** and move parties toward settlement.
- **Conflict prevention** — actions, policies and institutions that keep parties from using force; also preventing **recurrence** after conflict.
- **Post-conflict peace building** — multi-dimensional programmes addressing **root causes** to prevent **relapse**.
- **Preventive diplomacy** — skilled assistance (**good offices, mediation**) to resolve disputes **before they erupt** into armed conflict; also containing the spread of conflict.
- **Early warning** — systematic **collection and analysis of information** from crisis areas to **anticipate escalation** and present options to decision makers.
- **Interactive conflict resolution** — small-group **problem-solving discussions** between **unofficial representatives**, facilitated by an impartial third party.
- **Preventive (pre-conflict) peace building** — using **relief and development resources** to strengthen conflict-prone societies' capacity for non-violent resolution.
- **Preventive strategies — three principles:**
  1. **Early reaction**, which needs early detection and analysis
  2. A **comprehensive, balanced approach**
  3. An **extended effort on root causes**
  - Two sub-types: **operational prevention** (immediate crisis) and **structural prevention** (so crises don't arise or recur).

### Other Common Terms 🔑
- **Mediation** — negotiation helped by an **impartial, neutral third party** who has **no authority to decide** (unlike judges/arbitrators).
  - **Four stages:** (1) create the forum; (2) gather and share information; (3) problem-solving bargaining; (4) decision making.
- **Power mediation** — mediation **plus leverage** (promised **rewards** or threatened **punishment**).
- **Pure mediation** — facilitation through **reasoning, persuasion, information control and suggestion** only.
- **Reconciliation** — addressing the conditions behind conflict and turning **adversarial relationships into harmonious ones**; restores nationhood and **social capital**.
- **Reconstruction** — rebuilding physical infrastructure and the economy; post-conflict re-building:
  - **De-mining**, **disarmament**, **reintegration of combatants**
  - Return of refugees, resettlement of **IDPs**
  - Reviving politics and the economy, supervised elections
- **Capacity building** — developing individual and collective abilities, and institutions, to turn conflict into a **positive, constructive force**.
- **Compromise** — a settlement where **each party makes concessions**.
- **Conciliation** — two sides **voluntarily** agree to a compromise. Unlike **arbitration**, the solution is **not binding/compelled**.
- **Conciliator** — a **trusted third party** who provides a **communication link**, lowers tension and moves the parties toward direct negotiation.

### Case Study: Niger Delta Militancy and Federal Response 🔑
- The Niger Delta provides **80% of national wealth** (oil).
- **Niger Delta states (9):** **Abia, Akwa Ibom, Bayelsa, Cross River, Delta, Edo, Imo, Ondo, Rivers**.
- **Grievances:** extreme poverty, environmental degradation, unemployment, water pollution, **government neglect** → agitation for a bigger oil share and **resource control** → youth **militancy** and criminality.
- **Classified as a Protracted Social Conflict (PSC).**
- **Explained by the frustration–aggression theory** 🔑:
  - Proposed by **John Dollard and associates (1938)**: aggression **always presupposes frustration**, and frustration leads to aggression.
  - When people are blocked from their goals, frustration turns to aggression once **triggered**. It is aimed at the cause, or **displaced** onto another target (**Berkowitz 1980**).

**Four categories of federal response** 🔑
1. **Military/police deployment ("repression")**
   - Pro-active or pre-emptive deterrence.
   - **Criticised** for **militarising** the region and **escalating** militancy.
2. **Preventive peace building: constitutional/institutional bodies**

| Year | Body | Legal basis / notes |
|---|---|---|
| **June 1959** | **NDDB** — Niger Delta Development Board | Order-in-Council; initially for 10 years; **S.14 Nigerian (Constitution) Order-in-Council 1960**; **NDDB Act 1961**. **Failed** |
| **Aug 1976** | **NDBDA** — Niger Delta Basin Development Authority | **Decree 37 of 1976**; to tackle floods, pollution, erosion. **Failed**: government admitted oil caused the problems but **would not interfere with the oil industry** |
| **1992** | **OMPADEC** — Oil Mineral Producing Areas Development Commission | **Decree 23 of 1992** (Babangida); got **3% of the Federation Account** revenue. An improvement because it addressed real issues with statutory funds |
| **1999** | **NDDC** — Niger Delta Development Commission | **NDDC Act 1999, S.7(1)**. Covered all issues **except** the demand for **≥50% derivation** and resource control. Undermined by leadership struggles and funding seen as "**tokenism**" (**Briggs 2007**) |

3. **Post-conflict peace building: fiscal redistribution (derivation)**
   - Derivation fell from **50% (1951)** to **1.5%** (Buhari regime).
   - Raised to **3% in June 1992** (Babangida).
   - **Ecological fund** raised from **1% to 2%** of the Federation Account.
   - **1991:** NNPC and joint-venture oil firms must give **3% of capital investment** to host-community development.
   - **13% derivation** (set under the **Abacha** regime; **S.162(2), 1999 Constitution**, in force **29 May 1999**).
4. **Amnesty (preventive diplomacy)** — a political "**soft landing**" instead of a full military siege.
   - **1967:** **Gen. Yakubu Gowon** pardoned **Isaac Adaka Boro** and his **Niger Delta Volunteer Force (NDVF)** comrades, sentenced to death for their **February 1966** insurgency.
   - **October 2004:** **Obasanjo** signed a peace accord with **Asari Dokubo** (**NDPVF**, Niger Delta People's Volunteer Force) and **Tom Ateke** (**NDV**, "Niger Delta Volunteers" in the book; commonly known as Niger Delta Vigilante).
     - Terms: general pardon, disarmament and demobilisation; the government promised resettlement and **4,000 jobs** (**capacity building**).
     - Over **5,000 weapons** were surrendered (Oct 2004 – June 2005), but the government **failed to deliver** resettlement and jobs.
     - Asari Dokubo was **arrested in September 2005**. The militants regrouped as **MEND** (**Movement for the Emancipation of the Niger Delta**): sabotage, oil theft, pipeline destruction, guerrilla warfare, **kidnapping**.
   - **29 May 2007:** **President Umaru Musa Yar'Adua** took office, with **Goodluck Jonathan** (a Niger Delta indigene) as Vice-President.
     - He set up a **45-man Technical Committee** to harmonise all reports, from the **1957 Sir Henry Willink Commission** to the **2007 Peace and Conflict Resolution Committee**.
     - **25 June 2009: amnesty declared**, with a deadline of **4 October 2009** for disarmament.
     - About **15,000 militants** accepted → **relative peace**.

**Conclusion of the case study**
- The Niger Delta question is **not permanently resolved**.
- Granting full **resource control** would tilt economic power to the region and risk the **disintegration** of Nigeria. The conflict has become **intractable**.
- So the best achievable is **efficient conflict management**, not permanent resolution.

**Likely questions:** Origins of peace research vs conflict studies; Coser's definition; Wallensteen's types; agency vs structural theories (Galtung's structural violence); PSC (Azar) and CPE (Goodhand and Hulme); negative vs positive peace; conditions for positive peace; peacekeeping vs peace enforcement vs peace building; mediation stages; power vs pure mediation; conciliation vs arbitration; Niger Delta bodies (NDDB, NDBDA, OMPADEC, NDDC) with dates; derivation percentages; frustration–aggression theory; the 2009 amnesty.

## CHAPTER 14 — Information and Communications Technology and Social Sciences in the 21st Century (A.S. Jegede)

### Introduction
- ICT is part of daily life and important in **teaching and learning**. Teaching and learning are **relationship issues**, so ICT belongs in the social sciences.
- **All disciplines rest on the oars of the social sciences**, because no discipline is independent of human beings.

**Learning outcomes (match the SAQs):**
1. Difference between natural and social sciences
2. What ICT is
3. Relevance of ICT to the social sciences
4. Role of ICT in teaching and learning the social sciences

### Science, Natural Sciences and Social Sciences 🔑
- **Science** = the application of **systematic methods** to obtain knowledge, and the knowledge so obtained. Ancient explanations mixed observation with **magic and superstition**.
- **Natural sciences:** explain and predict events in the **natural environment**.
  - Main fields: **biology** (botany, zoology), **geology** (mineralogy, geomorphology), **chemistry** (organic, inorganic), **physics** (biophysics, quantum mechanics).
  - Each studies a "**slice**" of nature.
- **Social sciences:** examine **human relationships**; they try to **objectively understand the social world**. Its ordered relationships are not obvious and must be revealed by **repeated observation**.
  - Fields: **anthropology, economics, political science, psychology, sociology**.
- **SAQ 1.1 answer:** natural sciences explain and predict events in the natural environment objectively; social sciences examine human relationships and try to objectively understand the social world.

### What is ICT? 🔑
- The term **ICT replaced "IT" in the early 1990s**, in recognition of the computer's **communicating abilities** (**Adesoki and Fatoki 2013**).
- **Clarke (2006):** ICT covers "**a whole range of applications, techniques and systems** such as **computers, communications equipment and the services associated with them**." (This is the **SAQ 1.2 answer**.)
- ICT is also a **skill for life**, like literacy: needed to live confidently and independently in modern society.

### Three Positions of ICT in Education 🔑
1. **Learning about ICT** — ICT is the **subject** of learning in the curriculum.
2. **Learning with ICT** — ICT is a **medium to facilitate instruction**.
3. **Learning through ICT** — ICT is **integrated as an essential tool**, so the course **can no longer be taught without it**.

### Relevance of ICT to the Social Sciences 🔑
- ICT is now a **learning tool**, a **platform of interaction** between groups, and a form of communication that is fast becoming a **universal language**.
- Computer knowledge helps manpower in the **production and distribution** of goods and services. Employability depends on computer skills (e.g. **banking**).
- **Political science:** computers **analyse voting behaviour** and enable **prediction via pre-election surveys**. The **card reader** in Nigerian elections is ICT-based; advanced democracies show results as votes are cast.
- **SAQ 1.3 answers:** (1) ICT helps our understanding of the social sciences; (2) computer knowledge helps manpower in producing and distributing goods and services.

### ICT Literacy 🔑
- **Alvin Toffler:** "**The illiterate of the 21st century will not be those who cannot read and write, but those who cannot learn, unlearn, and relearn.**" 🔑
- **Haddad and Jurich (2002) — four basic issues** in the use of ICT in education: **effectiveness, cost, equity (equality), sustainability**. 🔑 ICTs (especially **computers and the internet**) can improve the efficiency and effectiveness of education at all levels, formal and non-formal.

### Roles of ICT in Teaching and Learning Social Sciences 🔑
- Affects **three areas:** the **teacher**, the **learner**, and the **image of social science** as a discipline.
- **Shift in curricula:** from conventional, **content-centred** teaching (lectures and tutorials, Kamal and Banu 2010) to **competency/performance-based** curricula (Stephenson 2001).
- **Competency-based curricula require:**
  - Access to varied information sources, forms and types
  - **Student-centred** settings based on inquiry
  - **Problem-centred, inquiry-based** activities
  - Authentic settings and examples
  - **Teachers as coaches and mentors**, not just content experts

**1. ICT and information literacy skills**
- **Information literacy** = "**the capacity to identify an issue and then to identify, locate and evaluate relevant information in order to engage with it or to solve a problem arising from it**" (**McCausland, Wache and Berk 1999**). 🔑
- Traditional **generic skills:** formal reasoning, problem solving, communication, negotiation, time and project management, collaboration and teamwork. Information literacy has been **added** to this pool.

**2. ICT and students' independent learning**
- Move from **teacher-centred** to **student-centred** delivery; students take **responsibility for their own learning**.
- Learning used to be by **transmission**. Now it is **problem-based learning** and the **Web as an information source**.
- ICT is a **catalyst for change**; computers become **information sources and cognitive tools** (**Jonassen and Reeves 1996**).

**3. ICT and alternative learning theories — Constructivism** 🔑
- Today's dominant theories are **constructivist** (**Duffy and Cunningham 1996**): learning is the **active construction of knowledge** from various perspectives in meaningful contexts.
- **Social interaction** plays a critical role in learning.
- Old model: **transmission** of knowledge, then interaction to consolidate it. New model: instruction **supports knowledge construction**.
- Learning = **construction of meaning**, not **memorisation of facts** (Lebow 1993).

- **SAQ 1.4 answers (three roles):** (1) information literacy skills; (2) students' independent learning; (3) social science research.

### Factors Favouring the Use of ICT 🔑
1. **Availability of funds** (Nwankwoala 2015).
2. **Availability of infrastructure** — equipment, multimedia, **adequate power supply** (Kessy, Kaemba and Gachoka 2006).
3. **Positive attitude to technological change** — teachers' attitudes are **major predictors** of use (**Almusalam 2001**); positive attitude plus **constructivist beliefs** (Sang, Valcke et al. 2010).
4. **Competency in ICT operation** — **Berner (2003)** tested **seven variables**; **belief in one's own computer competence** was the **greatest predictor** of classroom computer use.
5. **Personal characteristics** (age, years of experience) — **younger, less experienced teachers** use computers more (US Department of Education 2000). In Nigeria, some **old lecturers feel ICT is for the younger generation** (Nwankwoala 2015).

### Factors Hindering the Use of ICT 🔑
1. **Inadequate funding** — low government funding of Nigerian universities; the **internet is expensive to maintain**.
2. **Inadequate infrastructure:**
   - Computers are shared and must be **booked in advance**.
   - **Obsolete/poor-quality** resources; no **broadband**.
   - **Epileptic power supply**.
3. **Inadequate professional training** — few ICT technical staff to train lecturers (Mathipa and Mukhari 2014).
4. **Negative attitude to change** — preference for the **status quo** and face-to-face, teacher-centred teaching, which gives a "**sense of power**" (Makgato 2012). Some see ICT as a **waste of time** and skip workshops.
5. **Lack of technological know-how:**
   - **Pelgrum (2001):** the success of innovations depends on **teachers' skills and knowledge**.
   - Lack of knowledge → **lack of confidence → anxiety and fear of failure**.
   - Students may also lack skills because of scarce resources or **other priorities**.

### Advantages of ICT in Social Sciences 🔑
- ICT has made the world a **global village** (**Bhatti 2013**).
- **Fung (2013):** ICT affects **three phases of social science research**: **pre-data analysis, data analysis, post-data analysis**. 🔑

**In research:**
- **Pre-data analysis / literature review:**
  - Articles available online; **online libraries** with millions of digitised journals, books and theses; access **any time**.
  - **Safe storage**: can't be destroyed, stolen or misfiled.
  - **Exchange of ideas** across locations and disciplines.
  - **Mendeley** — software to **manage/track literature** (references). 🔑
- **Data analysis:**
  - **Qualitative:** **Dragon NaturallySpeaking** (transcribes recorded interviews); **NVivo** and **ATLAS.ti** (qualitative analysis). 🔑
  - **Quantitative:** **SPSS** (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences); no manual formulas, but you must know what results you need. 🔑
- **Post-data analysis:**
  - **Plagiarism detection** software.
  - **Online manuscript submission**; easy communication with **peer reviewers, editors and publishers**.

**In teaching:**
- Learning is **no longer limited by time and place** (Talebian, Mohammadi and Rezvanfar 2014).
- Collaboration; online resources; **flexibility** and **student-centredness** (CERI/OECD 2008); connects learning to the **real world** and builds confidence.
- Improves **quality of education**; **wider reach** via chat rooms and **video conferencing**.
- Gives lecturers more time for **personal development**.

### Disadvantages of ICT 🔑
- **Research:** online resources (especially online libraries) need **continual updating**.
- **Teaching:**
  1. Online resources may be **inaccurate** or not aligned with the course.
  2. Hard to run and assess **practical sessions** online.
  3. **Limits face-to-face interaction**.
  4. Hard to **measure students' progression**.
  5. Hard to **authenticate** that the registered student is the one participating.
  6. **Internet access** may not be available to all students.

### Chapter Summary
- There is a difference between natural and social sciences.
- ICT covers a **wide range of applications**.
- ICT is relevant to the social sciences.
- ICT plays a key role in teaching and learning the social sciences.
- **Revision questions given in the book:**
  1. Identify factors **favouring and hindering** ICT use.
  2. Identify **five importances** of ICT to the social sciences: ICT literacy; understanding basic issues in its use; teaching and learning; information literacy; independent learning; alternative learning theories.
  3. Discuss the **advantages and disadvantages** of ICT in the social sciences.

**Likely questions:** Natural vs social sciences; define ICT (Clarke); when ICT replaced IT; the three positions of ICT in education; Toffler quote; Haddad and Jurich's four issues; definition of information literacy; constructivism; factors favouring and hindering ICT; Fung's three research phases; named software (Mendeley, Dragon NaturallySpeaking, NVivo, ATLAS.ti, SPSS); advantages and disadvantages.
